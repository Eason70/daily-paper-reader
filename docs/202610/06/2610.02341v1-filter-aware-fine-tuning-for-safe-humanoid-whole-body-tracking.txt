Filter-Aware Fine-Tuning for Safe Humanoid Whole-Body Tracking
Pranit Mohnot1, Christian Helten2, Daniele Gammelli1,3, Marco Pavone1,4
Abstract— Safe whole-body motion is essential for deploying
humanoid robots in unstructured environments. Modern hu-
manoid control commonly separates reference specification from
execution, with a planner, teleoperator, or motion generator
providing a reference that a reinforcement-learning policy tracks
through dynamically feasible whole-body control. Runtime
safety filters, such as control barrier functions (CBFs), offer a
promising approach for enforcing newly introduced constraints
via interventions on the tracker’s outputs. We show, however,
that treating the tracking policy and safety filter independently
induces fundamental mismatches, as filtering alters both the
executed actions and the induced state distribution. We study this
policy–filter interface through case studies that isolate dynamics,
objective, and information mismatches, highlight their root
causes, and use these insights to develop CoFiT (Constrained
Filter-aware Tuning), a filter-aware fine-tuning method for
pretrained trackers. Across diverse constraint scenes, CoFiT
reduces violation time relative to filter-only training by 91% on
TWIST2 and 21% on SONIC, while requiring smaller safety
filter corrections. On Unitree G1 hardware, CoFiT reduces viola-
tion time by 83% for TWIST2 and completes every trial without
operator intervention, whereas 50% of baseline trials require an
operator stop. Together, these results provide actionable insights
into policy–filter interactions and establish design principles for
integrating learned trackers with runtime safety filters.
I. INTRODUCTION
Humanoid robots are rapidly moving toward deployment
in homes, warehouses, hospitals, and other human-centered
environments, where safe operation requires coordinating the
full body under task- and environment-dependent constraints.
Whole-body control increasingly relies on learning-based
motion tracking, in which a motion source, such as a
teleoperator, recorded demonstration, or kinematic motion
generator, provides a reference trajectory that a reinforcement-
learning (RL) tracking policy translates into joint commands
consistent with the robot’s dynamics [1], [2], [3]. While this
control architecture enables expressive whole-body motion,
ensuring its safety in real-world environments remains
challenging. A reference trajectory specifies the intended
motion but does not, in general, encode every constraint
of the environment in which that motion will be executed.
Obstacles, workspace limits, and other safety requirements
may be absent from the motion source, emerge only at
runtime, or change after the tracking policy has been trained.
Two broad strategies have emerged for enforcing safety
in whole-body control. The first internalizes safety within
the learned tracking policy, for example by training with
penalties on constraint violations or barrier-based rewards
that encourage avoidance of unsafe states [4]. This approach
tightly couples safety to training, as accommodating a new

Control barrier functions (CBFs) are widely used to

a condition on the control input, and the filter modifies the
tracker’s proposed command only as much as needed to satisfy
that condition [5]. Whole-body formulations can additionally
account for contact constraints and task priorities when
selecting the correction [7]. This modularity is appealing, as
the tracker can be treated as a fixed nominal controller, while
the filter supplies policy-agnostic corrections, seemingly
allowing the two components to be designed independently.
In this work, we show that treating the tracking policy and
safety filter independently induces fundamental mismatches in
closed-loop execution (Fig. 1). The safety filter changes the
tracker’s outputs even though the tracker was trained under an
unfiltered closed loop. As a result, both the actions executed
by the robot and the states induced by those actions differ from
those encountered during training. Through systematic case
studies, we identify three key sources of incompatibility at
the policy–filter interface: dynamics mismatch, objective mis-
match, and information mismatch. Guided by these findings
and their underlying causes, we develop CoFiT (Constrained
Filter-aware Tuning), a method that adapts a pretrained tracker
to an existing CBF filter. CoFiT fine-tunes the tracker with
filters active and supplies it with compact constraint infor-
mation, temporal feedback, and constraint-aware penalties.
This allows the tracker to anticipate filter interventions and
respond with coordinated, constraint-compatible motion while
retaining its original tracking objective where possible.

II. RELATED WORK
We review prior work along two complementary directions:
learning in the presence of runtime safety filters, and
incorporating safety constraints across the control hierarchy
for legged and humanoid robots.
A. Learning with safety filters
Safety filters separate nominal task execution from explicit
safety enforcement by admitting or modifying a policy’s
proposed actions according to safety constraints. CBF-based
methods enforce local model-based inequality constraints
on proposed controls, while predictive safety filters evaluate
candidate inputs through backup trajectories and terminal
safe sets [8], [6]. Several works train policies directly on
the resulting filtered system so that they can adapt to these
interventions. CBF-RL combines training-time filtering with
rewards based on barrier conditions and filter corrections [9],
while Safety Filtering While Training learns from filtered
transitions and penalizes corrective interventions with a
predictive filter retained during evaluation [10]. Related
approaches differentiate through CBF layers [11] or expose
barrier and Lie-derivative quantities to the policy when
selecting CBF-related parameters [12]. These methods show
that policies can benefit from being trained with knowledge
of the safety mechanism, but they do not address how a
pretrained whole-body tracker should be adapted to an already
deployed runtime filter, nor which constraint representations
and feedback signals are most useful for doing so. Our work
targets this setting by conditioning the nominal whole-body
tracker on a compact action-space representation of constraint
information and systematically studying how this constraint
information, along with temporal feedback and training
objectives, affects behavior under the inherited filter.
B. Constraints across the control hierarchy
Safety constraints can be incorporated at multiple levels
of a legged or humanoid control stack, including motion
planning, reference generation, and whole-body command
execution. Planning and tracking layers can share CBF
constraints, while filters above learned locomotion controllers
can modify high-level references [13], [14]. At the command
level, humanoid controllers combine barrier constraints with
full dynamics and contact variables [15], [16]. Task-consistent
formulations further account for how safety corrections
affect the primary motion and posture, as in OSCBF for
manipulation and ConstrainedMimic for pretrained humanoid
tracking [7], [17]. Other works study the role of safety-related
perception. For example, PAC-MAN combines depth with
barrier-based rewards for reactive humanoid dodgeball [4].
Collectively, these works establish effective mechanisms for
injecting safety constraints at different levels of the control
hierarchy, but largely treat the nominal tracking policy
and downstream whole-body filter as separately designed
components. Our work focuses on this interface: we retain the
existing whole-body safety filter and study how the tracking
policy that supplies its nominal commands should be adapted
to account for the filter’s constraints and interventions.

IV. FAILURE MODES OF INDEPENDENTLY
DESIGNED POLICIES AND SAFETY FILTERS
Base trackers were trained with a nominal unconstrained
model of the actuators, but applying filters at runtime
introduces a distribution shift that impacts the tracking and
stability performance of the underlying policy. We distinguish
three interacting mismatches in the composed policy-filter
loop, regarding dynamics, objective, and information.
A. Dynamics Mismatch
CBFs produce endogenous and autocorrelated perturbations
on unfiltered dynamics. Prior work has shown that CBF-QPs
can prevent convergence to a safe target by introducing stable
equilibria elsewhere [19], or maintain barrier satisfaction
while state components not directly constrained by the
barrier diverge [20]. For whole-body humanoid tracking,
the corresponding concerns are whether enforcing clearance
preserves both progress toward the reference and whole-body
stabilization.
This can be particularly problematic. Dynamic balance
requires coordinated posture and ground support beyond
what a torso-clearance barrier specifies. The task-space
QP addresses this by distributing corrections across joints
while limiting changes to contact forces, motion, and
posture (Section III-B). But minimizing this instantaneous
intervention does not guarantee that the pretrained tracker will
maintain coordination in its subsequent response. Fig. 2 (4.80–
5.62 s) shows an illustration of why that response matters. The
torso-CBF interaction is followed by a sharp forward bend
and raised knee, unlike the unfiltered comparison. The raised
knee also highlights how a disturbed response can affect
the filter itself. Mismatched dynamics can cause frequent
and unexpected contact transitions, leading to increased
uncertainty over contact mode (and thus which support
dynamics model to use), which can further disturb balance [7].
With multiple distinct constraints, these issues compound.
In Fig. 2 (5.82 s), the hand encounters another constraint while
this whole-body response is still occurring. The motion used
to regain coordination is itself subject to correction, so the
tracker must recover under continuing intervention. By Fig. 2
(7.98 s), the robot is more upright but remains displaced from
its reference. This instability clearly motivates fine-tuning
with the filter active, to help the tracker learn coordinated
responses to interventions [10]. More subtly, this supports
pairing correction and motion histories to help the tracker
account for the compounding effects of filter interventions.
B. Objective Mismatch
A tracker’s reference pursuit can often be incompatible
with safety, and while the filter can suppress instantaneously
unsafe actions, it cannot guide the policy’s objective towards
safety. For example, in the forward reach of Fig. 1 (left), the
reference enters the obstacle. Keeping the hand outside yields
a tracking error that encourages the policy to reach forward
again. Prior work identifies such persistent controller–filter
opposition as a source of chattering and oscillations [10],
which we observe in our example. The baseline continues
forward with violent oscillations and displaces the ball. And,
even in cases without constraint violations (not pictured), we
observe pronounced jerky motion, illustrating how persistent
controller–filter opposition can compound the whole-body
disturbances discussed in Section IV-A. The task is therefore
not simply to prevent violation, but to accommodate the
blocked reach without repeatedly disrupting the coordination
needed for the rest of the motion.
Training on filtered transitions alone does not explicitly
reward the policy for reducing intervention. Under filter
constraints, different proposals can yield the same executed
command and execution-based reward [21]. For a filter-
blocked trajectory, this means that proposing motion further
into the restriction can be indistinguishable in that reward
from proposing less, even though more correction is required.

C. Information Mismatch
Simply rewarding compatibility does not tell the tracker
which actions the current constraints permit. Consider
a hypothetical variation of Fig. 1: hold the robot state,
reference, and complete native input history fixed, but place
the sphere just left or right of the upcoming hand path. Before
either placement elicits correction, the tracker produces the
same action distribution, although a lateral adjustment that
increases clearance in one scene decreases it in the other.
A penalty can favor avoiding intervention in both scenes, but
identical inputs give the policy no basis for distinguishing the
placements. Past corrections cannot supply that distinction
before either scene has changed a command. Thus, the
policies should be aware of constraint information, even
before the constraints become active. Further, the provided
information should allow the policy to discern which action
directions will lead to violation, as well as constraint state and
approach rate (for our example, how far the hand is from the
ball and how quickly it approaches). Further, to help alleviate
the dynamics mismatches discussed in Sec. IV-A, we should
supply the policy a history of this constraint information.

We measure safety by violation time, the percentage
of recorded samples with any negative physical clearance;
filter cooperation by correction RMS, the root-mean-square
L2 norm of joint-target corrections. Motion quality is
measured by residual acceleration as the RMS acceleration of
joint-position tracking error, computed by second differences,

A. Controlled Design Study on Sphere Scenes
We sample motions from TWIST2’s training corpus and
place spherical obstacles along their reference trajectories,
close enough that the tracker must accommodate them while
continuing the motion (see example in Fig. 2). We compare
29 configurations over three training seeds each to specify
the details of CoFiT’s observation, history, and reward design
(following the broad insights from Section IV), and report the
most informative comparisons. While we care about more than
safety, it remains the first priority. Thus, we use the training
violation-rate curves in Fig. 4 as our primary selection tool,
deferring to other metrics only as needed. Note that training
violations are calculated under exploration noise and domain
randomization, so their values are higher than and not directly
comparable to deterministic validation numbers in Table I.


What constraint information does the policy need? (Fig. 4a)
We ablate subsets of the observation package given in (5),
with the encoder, constraint history, and rewards fixed. We
find neither half of the affine constraint to be useful alone:
supplying only the condition margin ˆβ or only the action-
space constraint normal ˆv performs no better than supplying
no constraint information at all. Supplied together, they
substantially reduce training violations. The barrier value and
rate also help on their own: they describe the current clearance
and approach, but do not specify how candidate actions affect
the constraint. The complete package achieves the lowest
training violation among the tested subsets. It also reaches
lower violation, and does so faster, than the oracle-observation
baseline given complete world-frame knowledge of the
obstacles. These results support the joint use of constraint-
state and action-condition information in this setting.
How should constraint information be integrated? (Fig. 4b)
We compare ways of integrating the constraint observation
with rewards fixed and no constraint history. Attention
queries all the encoded constraints using the remaining
policy input. Softmax and max pooling aggregate all encoded
constraints. Top-k selects the k constraints with the smallest
barrier values and encodes them directly, zero-padding when
fewer valid constraints are available. Attention and softmax
yield the highest training violation rates. The remaining three
are essentially tied on training safety. We choose top-1 as
it is slightly safer in validation (Table I) and simpler.
What historical information is useful? (Fig. 4c)
Both base trackers are provided a short window of
proprioceptive history, which lets a policy infer unobserved
properties of its own dynamics from how the body responded
to recent commands [25], [26]. As discussed in Sec. IV-A, the
filter perturbs these dynamics. Holding the nearest-constraint
description and rewards fixed, we cumulatively add constraint
history, then correction history (the tracker already receives
the filtered previous action and a single step of the correction).
We find that each addition reduces violation time.
How should the reward be modified? (Fig. 4d)
We ablate the added penalties while retaining the complete
nearest-constraint description and its history. Penalizing the
task-space correction the filter applies (rc) captures most of
the available gain. Adding a penalty on proposals that violate
the condition before correction (rr) helps further (nontrivially).
Beyond those two, the benefits are incremental: three further
penalties, on the intermediate condition (ri), the realized
violation (rh), and the largest slack the solver allowed (rs),
land close to one another and close to the pair (Table I), with
ri marginally safest (each new reward was weighted to con-
tribute similarly). What matters is that the policy receive some
signal about what the filter had to do. We choose to use rs,
since it alone measures the solver’s behavior rather than the
robot’s state. A practitioner may reasonably choose otherwise.
The CoFiT recipe
Thus, these design ablations shape our chosen recipe
for CoFiT: the nearest constraint’s complete description,
histories of that description and of the filter’s corrections, and

B. Constrained Tracking Across Scenes and Policies
We next evaluate the CoFiT design on generated constraint
scenes by fine-tuning TWIST2 and SONIC separately.
We use a mix of manipulation, locomotion, and locoma-
nipulation reference motions generated via Kimodo [27],
conditioned on language descriptions of household tasks.
To generate scene geometry, we sweep the robot’s collision
bodies along each reference and place obstacles relative to the
resulting volumes, so every scene is built around the motion
it constrains. Obstacles are spheres, boxes, and capsules, each
paired with the bodies it restricts, drawn from free-space lay-
outs and kitchen geometry from RoboCasa [28] (see Fig. 5).
Unlike the sphere scenes, we let obstacles overlap the refer-
ence itself, so the tracker must depart from its prescribed ref-
erence to remain safe. We fine-tune each backbone separately
over three seeds, comparing CoFiT with observations-only
(our observation package with native rewards), penalties-only
(our added penalties without constraint observations), filter-
only fine-tuning, and continued training without filtering.2
We find that CoFiT improves constraint adherence and
policy-filter cooperation in both trackers. With the filter
active for all five conditions, CoFiT achieves the lowest
mean violation time and correction RMS in both trackers.
Relative to filter-only fine-tuning, violation time falls by
91% for TWIST2 and 21% for SONIC, and correction RMS
by 59% and 29%, respectively (Table II). The five treatments
also have the same ordering on both measures in the two
trackers (CoFiT, observations-only, penalties-only, filtered
training, and unfiltered training). Further, CoFiT provides
smooth motion, giving the lowest mean command variation
in both trackers, together with lower residual acceleration
than filter-only training.
The observation-only comparison explains much of this
improvement. In both trackers, observations-only outperforms
penalties-only on violation and correction. More importantly,
adding our observations to filter-only training improves
constraint adherence without higher mean world-frame
tracking error, while also reducing residual acceleration and
command variation, with the native rewards unchanged. The

C. Real-Robot Validation
Finally, we test whether the learned policy-filter interaction
improves execution on a physical Unitree G1 during a
3We hypothesize, but do not validate experimentally, that SONIC’s lack

prescribed reaching task near a basketball. For each backbone,
we evaluate CoFiT and the unfiltered-training baseline—both
trained on the generated scenes as in Sec. VI-B—as they
execute the same motion with the whole-body filter active
at 50 Hz. The reference enters the modeled exclusion sphere,
requiring the tracker to accommodate the blocked reach
while maintaining the rest of the whole-body motion. We
include a conservative safety buffer of approximately 4 cm
to allow for sim-to-real mismatch, giving a 32 cm-diameter
virtual obstacle. This buffer was fixed before the experiments.
For both trackers, the filter receives the pelvis and basketball
poses from motion capture (MoCap), together with robot
state for forward kinematics.
As before, our TWIST2-based policy receives the
additional global-position feedback, while SONIC does
not. Table III reports ten physical repetitions per condition.
One SONIC-CoFiT trial had unusable logged data but
completed without an operator stop. It is excluded from
continuous-metric statistics but included in the stop count.
With our TWIST2-based tracker on the G1, we demonstrate
improved constraint adherence and tracking together with


markedly lower residual acceleration and command variation.
CoFiT reduces violation time by 83% relative to continued
training, while lowering world-frame tracking error and
the correction required from the filter (Table III). Across
repeated trials, residual acceleration falls from 65.6 to
31.9 rad/s2, while command variation also falls by more
than half. The RMS keypoint-acceleration trace in Fig. 6(b)
shows much smoother motion for CoFiT than the baseline
through the illustrated reach, and the pose overlays in Fig. 1
show the resulting difference in whole-body motion. Five of
ten baseline TWIST2 trials required an e-stop due to violent
thrashing whereas CoFiT completed all ten without a stop.
Using SONIC on the same G1, we also find reductions
in correction RMS, residual acceleration, and reaching-arm
velocity error with CoFiT. Mean violation time falls from
7.64% to 5.24%. These gains accompany an increase in
world-frame MPJPE from 5.45 to 12.17 cm and slightly
higher command variation. 4
Both policies remain relatively steady in Fig. 6(a),
although the baseline makes light contact with the ball.
The corresponding acceleration traces in Fig. 6(c) show
an improvement in smoothness with CoFiT, though less
pronounced than for TWIST2. All ten CoFiT trials complete
without an operator stop, as do all ten continued-training trials.
Together, these hardware results show that the gains
in constraint adherence and filter cooperation extend to
physical execution on both trackers, with every CoFiT trial
completing without an operator stop.
VII. CONCLUSION
CoFiT fine-tunes humanoid trackers to improve constraint
adherence and cooperation with runtime filters. We find that a
compact constraint description already computed by the filter
improves cooperation even under native rewards, with correc-
tion feedback and penalties providing further gains. The recipe
applies across both tested trackers and several obstacle geome-
tries. Future work will integrate CoFiT with safety-aware plan-
ners to help humanoids carry out more complex tasks safely.

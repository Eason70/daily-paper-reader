## Abstract 

Offline multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) faces a persistent trade-off. Expressive generative policies can represent multi-modal coordination in the data, but cannot distinguish high-value regions, while value-optimized policies exploit the learned Q-function but collapse the multi-modal into a single dominant mode. A single agent’s mode collapse can break joint coordination, and simultaneous drift across agents can push the joint policy into unseen regions of the action space. We propose scalable coordination via optimal unified transport (SCOUT) , the first offline MARL framework to combine a generative foundation model with a learned value function through test-time action refinement. SCOUT trains two decoupled components: a flow-matching behavioral prior and a decomposed value function. At test-time, it transports behavioral samples toward high-value regions via Stein variational gradient descent. The number of transport steps controls adaptive test-time scaling, replacing a fixed regularization coefficient. Under the individual-global-max (IGM) principle, we prove a single-term KL bound on the joint soft-value gap that vanishes as transport converges, with an irreducible additive residual proportional to the IGM violation. Empirically, SCOUT achieves the best average performance across discrete and continuous offline MARL benchmarks and yields performance improvements in all offline-to-online configurations.

## 1 Introduction 

Offline MARL is motivated by the need to learn coordinated multi-agent behaviors in safety-critical or cost-constrained domains where active data collection is infeasible or expensive, e.g. , autonomous driving [1], logistics [2], and robotics [3]. In this setting, the learning objective is twofold. Agents must learn to coordinate their behavior from heterogeneous and suboptimal demonstrations, while avoiding the distributional shift that arises when the learned policy visits state-action regions unsupported by the data. Early offline MARL methods [4–6] rely on simple policy classes ( i.e. , Gaussian), which struggle to represent multi-modal and coordinated joint action distributions. When offline data contains a mixture of diverse coordination strategies, a unimodal policy often collapses onto a single mode and produces out-of-distribution joint actions that no agent in the data ever executed together. To address this expressiveness gap, recent works adopt powerful generative models, including 

diffusion models [7, 8] and flow matching models [9, 10], as policy representations in offline MARL. Although these models improve the representational power of policy, their iterative sampling process incurs a high inference cost. MAC-Flow [10] and OM 2P [9] mitigate this via one-step distillation and mean-flow generation, respectively. However, improved expressiveness and faster inference do not resolve a more fundamental limitation. Since the outcome policy is optimized against a learned Q-function, it inherently tends to collapse diverse coordination modes into a single or blurred one. Methods that parameterize a policy and optimize it to maximize a learned Q-function must balance value improvement against staying close to the behavioral distribution. A fixed regularization coef-ficient controls this trade-off. If it is too large, the policy stays too close to suboptimal data; if it is too small, the policy exploits errors in the learned Q-function and produces OOD actions. Although this tension is well-known in single-agent offline RL [11, 12], the multi-agent setting introduces the following compounding difficulty. Training-time value maximization deforms each agent’s policy away from the behavioral prior, and the regularization coefficient bounds how far each marginal may drift, but cannot bound the compound effect on the joint. Even when every marginal remains within its individual support, the joint policy can place mass on action combinations that no trajectory in the data ever visited ( i.e. , regions where the learned Q-function is least reliable). In fact, the data may support multiple distinct joint modes of comparably high estimated value, but committing each marginal to a single greedy mode at training time discards the others before the agent has any way to tell them apart. Once new interaction data arrives, the policy can no longer use it to discriminate which mode is truly higher-value, because the alternatives have already been suppressed. A natural alternative is to decouple two objectives, motivated by test-time training [13–15]. Instead of learning a policy that directly maximizes value, we keep the behavioral policy as a foundational prior and defer value-based refinement to test time. At deployment, we sample action candidates from this prior and then move them toward high-value regions using value gradients. The extent of this adjustment acts as adaptive control, replacing the need for a fixed regularization coefficient. In this work, we ask a question not yet studied by prior works: 

How do we avoid explicit policy parameterization for value maximization, and instead recover coordination at test time by directly transporting behavioral samples toward high-value regions? 

We answer this question with the proposed solution, SCOUT (scalable coordination via optimal unified transport) , an offline MARL framework that combines flow-matching behavioral priors with decomposed value gradient for test-time action refinement. Our key contributions are: • We propose a policy-free value maximization for offline MARL that separates behavioral modeling from value improvement, replacing training-time regularization tuning with a test-time refinement budget. • We derive decomposed value gradient transport under the IGM principle, enabling fully decentralized test-time refinement that recovers inter-agent coordination with a bounded approximation gap. • Empirically, test-time refinement strengthens coordination without retraining, achieving the best average performance in continuous and discrete domains. Notably, unlike existing flow-based MARL, our method yields improvements in all offline-to-online RL experiments. 22 Related Works 

Offline MARL. Several works have explored the data-driven sequential decision-making problem, 

including offline RL and MARL, which aims to extract a return-maximizing policy within the support of an offline dataset. Specifically, offline MARL requires learning coordinated policies for multiple agents. This is not trivial because it requires modeling dependencies among agents in a combinatorially large joint action space. The simplest baselines are multi-agent extensions of single-agent offline RL methods, either by introducing conservatism in the value function [11, 12] or the actor [16], or by performing Bellman updates without querying out-of-distribution actions [17]. To further promote coordination, more advanced works are often combined with additional multi-agent design choices, such as value decomposition [5, 18], policy optimization with a centralized critic [4, 6], or a centralized world model [19]. However, these methods often struggle to capture the multi-modal distributions present in multi-agent offline datasets because of their limited policy expressiveness. To address this issue, recent work has adopted more expressive generative policies, including an autoregressive model [20], diffusion models [7], and flow matching [10]. However, these methods couple value learning with the generative process itself: they either anchor the policy via BC-like losses [21, 22, 10, 23], which produces overly conservative behavior, or backpropagate value gradients through iterative sampling [24, 25], which is unstable. In contrast, the proposed solution bypasses explicit policy parameterization altogether. Instead, Stein variational gradient [26, 27, 14] fields refine flow-generated action candidates toward high-value regions at test time. This enables adaptive test-time scaling while retaining generative expressiveness. 

Test-time action refinement. Our main idea in this work is based on test-time refinement ( i.e. ,

a ← a + ∇aQ(s, a )) [13], which refines actions from a base generative policy at inference time rather than during training. This paradigm has been explored along several directions in single-agent RL. Rejection sampling methods generate multiple action candidates and select the highest-valued one [28, 29]. Gradient-based approaches directly optimize sampled actions via Q-function gradients [25, 14, 30]. Residual policies instead train a separate network to modify a base policy in noise or action space [31, 32]. Complementary to these, optimal transport (OT) offers a principled framework for moving probability mass toward high-value regions [33, 34]. More recently, particle-based methods adopt an OT viewpoint to transport samples toward value-preferred regions [35]. However, these approaches remain limited to single-agent settings. The proposed solution is motivated by this direction but specifically designed to solve the multi-agent coordination problem. This avoids explicit policy parameterization and naturally handles the combinatorial joint action space.

. (6) 44 Scalable Coordination via Optimal Unified Transport ( SCOUT )

As motivated in Section 1, our goal is to develop an offline MARL algorithm that can extract Q-maximizing multi-agent policies without explicit policy parameterization for value maximization. Following prior works [14, 56, 57], we consider the MaxEnt surrogate of (2), Ea∼π [Q(s, a )] + 

αH(π(·| s)) , whose optimal policy takes the Boltzmann form π∗(a|s) ∝ exp( Q(s, a )/α ).Rather than fitting an explicit actor to approximate π∗, we construct a transport map that moves sam-ples from μ toward π∗ via variational gradient descent, using ∇aQ as the driving force. Specifically, given a set of action candidates sampled from a flow-matching behavioral policy, we iteratively refine them at test time via the simplest possible update as a ← a + η∇aQ(o, a ). The resulting refined actions constitute the implicit policy output, with the number of gradient steps L serving as a tunable knob that controls coordination strength at deployment without retraining. 

Why do we need an implicit policy? This direction offers several appealing properties. First, because the initial actions are drawn from the behavioral policy, the implicit policy refines the behavioral prior rather than replacing it. Then, the transport budget provides an adaptive test-time scaling. When 

L = 0 , it recovers the best sampling from the behavioral policy, while increasing L progressively pushes actions toward Q-maximizing regions. Furthermore, the gradient-based transport can discover actions beyond the reference distribution when the Q-function generalizes well. 

4.1 Why is this hard in multi-agent settings? 

The above idea and problem hold straightforwardly in the single-agent setting [14, 48], where the transport operates on a single action space A guided by a single Q-function. However, extending this to multi-agent settings is non-trivial. Under the CTDE paradigm, each agent i can only access its local observation oi at test time. This means the action refinement must run independently for each agent, using only its local Q-function Qi(oi, a i) and its own action particles. The refined joint action

This tension between decentralized execution and coordinated behavior has been a long-standing challenge in MARL [39, 40]. The most common solution is the IGM principle [39] in (1), which ensures independent per-agent maximization to align with joint maximization. However, several practical questions remain. First, IGM guarantees alignment at the greedy argmax, but the gradient-based transport involves iterative updates that modify the particle distribution across multiple steps. It is unclear whether the per-agent transport dynamics remain aligned with the joint optimum throughout this process, especially when the critic must evaluate actions that are progressively shifted away from the behavioral support during training. Second, the individual behavioral prior might discard the inter-agent correlations present in the offline data. Even if the transport dynamics are correctly factorized, the initial particles may lack the coordination patterns necessary for effective joint behavior.

4.4 Algorithm summary 

We summarize the SCOUT framework in Pseudocode 1. Training alternates between two decou-pled objectives: ( i) the flow-matching loss LBC fits the individual behavioral prior with ODE in Pseudocode 2, and the TD loss Lcritic learns decomposed Q functions whose targets are computed through the additive decomposition Qtot = P 

> i

Qi using actions refined by Ltrain transport steps in Pseudocode 3. At test time, each agent independently generates P behavioral candidates via ODE integration, refines them for Ltest steps of decomposed value gradient flow, and selects the highest-Qi particle. The transport budget (Ltest , ϵ ) need not match training. In particular, Ltest = 0 recovers best-of-P

sampling, Ltest = Ltrain matches training conditions, and Ltest > L train enables additional test-time scaling when the Q-function generalizes beyond the data support.

## 6 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose SCOUT , the first test-time policy refinement framework for offline MARL that decouples behavioral modeling from value maximization. While prior generative policies often compromise expressiveness by integrating value guidance during training, SCOUT preserves the multi-modal behavioral prior and defers value-driven refinement strictly to test-time inference via decomposed Stein variational gradient transport. We provide a single-term KL bound on the joint soft-value gap under approximate IGM and constructively explain how additive TD targets transfer inter-agent dependencies into per-agent gradients. 

Limitation. While decoupling the behavioral prior from value maximization is effective across a range of tasks, the refinement hyperparameters exhibit some task- and dataset-dependent sensitivity. Therefore, a principled adaptive stopping criterion remains an open direction for future work.

B.1 Computational resources 

All experiments were conducted on a single server equipped with an Intel Xeon E5-2650 v3 CPU and an NVIDIA A40 GPU. The software stack consists of CUDA 12.2, cuDNN 8.9.7, Python 3.9, and JAX 0.4.30. 

B.2 Implementation details Flow BC prior. We instantiate the foundation as a conditional flow-matching model that learns the behavior distribution of the offline dataset. Given an observation and a Gaussian noise sample, the actor parameterizes a velocity field that transports x0 to a data action x1 along the linear interpolation, trained by minimizing the flow-matching loss. At inference time, candidate actions are produced by a fixed-step Euler integration of the learned ODE, so the prior remains an explicit, multimodal generative model of the dataset rather than a unimodal collapse. Because the resulting samples are biased toward the modeled behavioral support, they encourage the agent to remain near in-distribution behaviors, while still preserving the diverse coordination patterns present in the offline corpus. 

Sinusoidal time embedding. The flow conditioning variable t ∈ [0 , 1] carries information about how close the current sample xt is to data; feeding it as a raw scalar concatenated to the network input yields poor expressivity, since a single-channel signal interacts weakly with high-dimensional weights and forces the actor to learn very different behaviors at neighboring t values from a one-dimensional cue.

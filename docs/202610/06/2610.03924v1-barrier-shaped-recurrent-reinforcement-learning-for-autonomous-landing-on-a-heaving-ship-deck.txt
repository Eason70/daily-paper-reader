# Barrier-Shaped Recurrent Reinforcement Learning for Autonomous Landing on a Heaving Ship Deck 

Ritwik Shankar *,1 , Chiranjeev Prachand *,2 , Abhishek 1, and Soumya Ranjan Sahoo 2

Abstract —This paper addresses autonomous landing of un-manned aerial vehicle (UAV) rotorcrafts on a heaving ship deck using a recurrent policy trained via asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning: the critic sees 6 s of future deck height during training, while the actor sees only what the onboard sensors provide in flight, a 17-dimensional state made of the vehicle’s position, velocity and attitude relative to the deck and outputs world-frame velocity and yaw-rate commands. Training is performed across 4096 parallel simulated environments, fol-lowed by fine-tuning in 16 environments with an onboard vision pipeline in the loop. A control-barrier-function (CBF) stopping margin on the deck-relative vertical state is incorporated at two stages: as a reward term during training, where it halves the median simulated contact speed relative to a policy trained without it, and as a runtime safety filter at deployment, evaluated at every control step to abort and retry the descent when the margin is violated. Because the autopilot’s disarm logic cannot detect the vehicle resting on a moving deck, proximity-based thrust cutoff at touchdown is commanded directly in the landing pipeline. The proposed approach is validated using a parallel-manipulator-platform-based deck emulator that reproduces the heaving motion of the ship deck (scaled to 0.70 m peak-to-peak, 

7.5 s mean period) and a quadcopter UAV with an onboard camera. Across 31 motion-capture and 20 vision-based trials, the UAV landed every time, with median times to contact of 6.5 and 

7.3 s ; 77% and 50% landed on the first attempt, with mean deck-relative speeds of 0.29 and 0.27 m /s, respectively, at the instant

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Autonomous landing of a vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) capable unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) on a moving ship deck matters for maritime logistics and naval recovery. The vehicle must track a deck that translates and rotates, choose when to descend, and touch down within tight limits on relative velocity, attitude, and deck size. For ship-deck motions such as roll (about X), pitch (about Y ), sway (along Y ), and surge (along X), the vehicle can null the relative translation it currently observes, and it can decide to stop or continue the descent according to the tilt it observes at that moment. That observation is a sufficient statistic for landing. Early deck-landing systems accordingly focused on recovering the relative deck pose and controlling the vehicle’s angular and horizontal motion. Monocular and marker-based methods established this pipeline on laboratory motion platforms, simulated decks, and wave-perturbed small surface vessels [1]–[4]. These systems established the perception and feedback required for landing under observable angular and lateral deck motion. The quantity that determines contact is now the relative velocity at an instant that has not yet arrived, so the current relative pose no longer determines the correct action. A constant descent makes the difficulty concrete. Taking every sample of a measured ship-heave record [5] as an instant at which the vehicle could arrive, and descending at a constant 0.5 m /s,the rate an off-the-shelf autopilot uses to land, the relative speed at contact exceeds 0.5 m /s at half of them, because the deck is rising as often as it is falling. A landing attempt must therefore be timed against motion that has not yet occurred, and a descent must be abandoned once safe contact can no longer be achieved. The landing must also end by itself. An autopilot disarms only after the airframe stops moving, and a heaving deck keeps it moving, so the vehicle would sit on the deck with its motors still running and never disarm. The policy therefore cuts the thrust itself, once it is within 

7 cm of the deck. Cutting at contact instead is possible, since the camera gives the relative height, but a vehicle that is still producing thrust when the gear meets a moving deck can slide or tip before it settles. Dropping the last few centimeters lets it arrive with enough weight on the gear to settle in place. This operational insight motivated the adoption of a proximity-based disarm logic. The policy steers the descent up to that instant, and the vehicle falls unpowered after it, so the speed the policy achieves and the speed at contact are two different numbers. Classical approaches decompose this task into estimation, motion prediction, descent planning, and safety logic. Pre-cision landing on a vertically oscillating platform has been achieved by tracking its height [6] and by coupling a UKF estimate of known multi-sinusoidal motion to time-optimal trajectory generation [7]. Such pipelines rest on a forecast of the ship’s motion, from Kalman estimators [8]–[10] to learned time-series models [11]–[13], coupled to a planned descent [14]. In maritime experiments, an FFT–Kalman ob-server and MPC reached the deck near zero roll and pitch with a forecast of about 1 s , treating vertical motion as negligible and scoring success on tilt alone [15]; a later system from the same group added onboard vision and landed on a rocking USV, reporting its hardware trials as roll and pitch [16]. Distributed MPC has selected a calm patch of water and a low-tilt touchdown time from a known wave model, in a two-dimensional simulation with a communicating vessel [17]. Prediction-driven implementations still require an explicit forecast, a hand-designed phase structure, and a tuned descent trigger. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a route to coordinating the coupled tracking and timing objectives within a policy. Landing policies have progressed from visual touchdown and inclined surfaces to continuous tracking of horizontally moving pads [18]–[22]. End-to-end image-based landing with learned three-axis velocity commands has been demonstrated on a ground vehicle following a horizontal path at up to 

1 m /s; the study identifies memory as a requirement for more complex platform motion [23]. Robust policies have also been trained for a sub-scale ship and wind disturbances [24]. That policy commands roll and pitch while a separate command executes the vertical landing, so heave stays outside the action space and the descent is not decided against deck motion. The closest learned heave systems broaden the motion and validation envelope. A policy that infers platform motion from two successive sparse visual observations and commands attitude and thrust was evaluated in 411 onboard trials on a Stewart platform and a wave-excited pool float [25]. The success criterion of Angelis et al. [25] allows 1.5 m /s of relative speed at contact. The two test platforms of Angelis et al. each exhibit one half of the problem: the Stewart platform moves 0.25 m but follows a single sine wave, and the pool float moves irregularly but only 0.13 m . The algorithm in our work does both at once, following recorded ship motion over 0.70 m 

peak to peak, and a touchdown is scored against a 0.5 m /s

bound on the relative speed. WaveLander [26] starts from the same premise as our work, learning when to descend, hold position, and retreat through a recurrent policy that commands a scalar vertical-velocity reference. The WaveLander policy is trained without privileged information; the policy in our work gives the critic future deck height that the actor never observes, so the descent decision is learned against motion that the policy itself cannot see. The WaveLander training model constrains the vehicle to one vertical prismatic joint and drives the deck with synthetic multi-sinusoidal motion plus correlated noise; our work considers ship-heave motion [5], which is not purely periodic in nature. In WaveLander, lateral tracking is a fixed controller; success is judged on attitude, and one deployment test is reported. In our work, the policy commands the lateral channels itself and is flown 51 times on a physically heaving deck emulator platform. The literature leaves three gaps. First, heave timing is sel-dom learned from instantaneous measurements of ship motion. Second, policies are trained above a simulator-side controller that only approximates the autopilot that will fly them; putting the real autopilot in the loop fixes the mismatch, but runs one vehicle at a time [27], which rules out the parallel training that these policies need. Third, learned maritime landing algorithms reach deployment without a runtime safety check on the descent they commit to [24]–[26]. This paper closes all three gaps. The deck position a few seconds ahead decides the landing. A vehicle in flight cannot know it, but the simulator does, because it replays a recorded sea state. Only the critic is given that future deck height, and only during training. The actor is recurrent, so it reads the state of the deck from what it has just seen and decides when to descend. No forecast window or peak-selection rule is prescribed. A feed-forward actor sees a single observation and cannot do this, so the privileged critic has nothing to teach it; feed-forward runs did not learn to land, and the two are not compared here. For the second gap, the policy is trained above a Torch implementation of the PX4 cascade [28], and it (a) (b)

The deployed autopilot takes those same four channels, so the interface does not change between simulation and flight. For the third gap, a control-barrier-function (CBF) [29] filter wraps the deployed policy in an outer loop and checks the vertical velocity it commands at every tick. The check reads the deck-relative vertical state and passes only while a feasible stopping margin remains. Once no margin remains, the filter overrides the command and aborts to hover, so a descent that has run out of stopping margin becomes a retry. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first learned ship-deck landing policy trained with privileged future deck motion and checked at run time by a control-barrier-function monitor on flight hardware. The barrier is used in its standard role, and the work makes no claim of a new safe-learning theory. This work combines these elements and demonstrates their use for landing a vehicle on a physically heaving deck. The main contributions of this work are as follows: 1) a recurrent landing policy for a heaving deck, trained with an asymmetric actor–critic, where the critic sees the next 6 s of deck motion and the actor only the deck-relative state available onboard, and which commands the autopilot through its own velocity interface; 2) a control-barrier-function filter around the deployed pol-icy that aborts and retries when the stopping margin is exhausted, with the same margin used as a training re-ward, which halves the median simulated contact speed against the same policy trained without it; 3) 51 hardware landings on a deck replaying measured ship heave at 0.70 m peak to peak, 31 under motion capture and 20 under onboard vision, all successful, with 97 % and 95 % of disarms inside 0.5 m /s; the disarm speed the policy controls and the contact speed the airframe absorbs are reported separately, and the unpowered fall between them is measured. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Sec. II defines the landing task and describes the policy’s observation, action, architecture, and training. Sec. III develops the barrier on the deck-relative vertical state and the runtime check and retry it drives. Sec. IV describes the vehicle, the deck emulator, and the criteria by which a landing is judged. Sec . V presents the simulation benchmark of five controllers and the hardware study of 51 landings under motion capture and onboard vision. Sec. VI concludes and sets out the future work. II. H EAVE -L ANDING TASK AND POLICY 

A. Task Definition 

With world-frame states pu, vu for the UAV and pp, vp for the platform, the deck-relative state is prel = pu − pp and 

vrel = vu − vp; the platform translates only in heave. Each episode starts with the UAV above the deck, offset horizontally and slightly perturbed. It must align with the deck center, hold a level attitude, choose when to descend, and reach first contact within the horizon at a low deck-relative vertical speed. No future deck motion is available at deployment, so the descent decision comes from the observation history alone, making the heave landing a partially observable finite-horizon problem. Touchdown and failure conditions follow the training setup. Fig. 1 summarizes the task, the training loop, and the deployment path.

C. Training Setup Recorded deck motion and data split: The environment replays five 1800 s heave records (D1H1–D1H5, abbreviated D1–D5) from the SCONE dataset [5] at 20 Hz . D1, D2, D4, and D5 are used for training; D3 is used only for validation and testing. Every reset samples a training record, a start index, and a deck-height bias in [0 .5, 2.5] m. Each record is centered and normalized to [−1, 1] , so scaling by 0.35 gives 0.70 m 

commanded peak-to-peak heave. 

Simulation and action execution: Episodes reset with the UAV at 3.5 −5.0 m and x, y ∈ [−1, 1] m about the deck, with small attitude and velocity perturbations, and the platform moves in every environment. Domain randomization covers mass, action delay, the position, velocity, attitude, and projected-gravity estimates, per-rotor thrust scale, motor lag, and velocity-loop gains. A batched Torch re-implementation of a PX4-like multicopter cascade executes each velocity setpoint rather than applying it as a force, running with the physics at 

200 Hz beneath the 20 Hz policy, so training and deployment share the interface of eq. (2). 

Policy optimization: Training uses PPO [32] in RSL-RL with 4096 parallel Isaac Lab environments, 48 steps per environment and update, six mini-batches, five epochs, clip 0.2, γ = 0 .99 and λ = 0 .95 . Training from scratch takes about 3 h on a desktop with an NVIDIA RTX 5090, at which point the good-touchdown rate has converged. The same weights are then fine-tuned in a 16-environment run, with the deployed vision pipeline in the loop, so the actor acts on the filtered estimates it receives in real time.

Although all 51 trials reached the deck, only 49 satisfied this cutoff-speed criterion; the two disarms above 0.5 m /s were 0.510 m /s under motion capture, just over the bound, and 0.543 m /s in a vision run whose velocity estimate had failed and read zero through the descent. A barrier-initiated return to hover is not considered a failure but is recorded separately as a retry. The delayed velocity also explains the higher retry rate un-der vision. In ten of the vision landings, the barrier intervened once during the descent, returned the vehicle to hover, and the policy then landed on a second descent. For each intervention, the barrier was re-evaluated with the motion-capture state at the same instant. The true margin was still positive every time, between 0.06 and 0.37 m , so every intervention came early. In two of the ten, the true margin went negative within the next 0.6 s , so the intervention anticipated a violation the deck was about to produce; in the other eight, it never did, and the intervention was caused by the lagged velocity estimate alone. A retry costs a few seconds, and no landing was lost, so the filter erred on the safe side. The barrier’s guarantee holds for the state it is given: with exact relative height and velocity and braking within arel ,max , the class-K condition keeps h ≥ 0. In flight, its inputs arrive late or fail, so a filter for deployment must also account for the age and validity of its estimate; that is left to future work. Vision-in-the-loop fine-tuning did little to close the sensing gap. Rendering the camera and running the detector limited that stage to 16 environments, and raising the count cut training throughput sharply, so the policy saw far fewer vision samples than state samples. Future work will move the whole pipeline into the loop, either through feature tracking that yields the relative pose directly, as in [25], or by parallelizing the marker detector across environments. VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This research proposed a method for ship-deck landing of a rotorcraft on a heaving deck with an asymmetric actor-critic whose critic sees the future deck and whose actor does not, and verified the learned descent through real-time hardware deployment with a stopping-distance barrier. On a deck emulator replaying measured ship heave at 0.70 m peak to peak, all 51 trials landed. Under motion capture, 30 of 31 cut thrust within 0.5 m /s of the deck, 77 % of them on the first descent. Under onboard vision, 19 of 20 did so, half of them on the first descent, the retries coming from a delayed velocity estimate rather than from the policy. Four directions follow from this work. The emulator repro-duces deck kinematics alone, so future work will add air-wake and wind over the deck, and will replay scaled six-degree-of-freedom sea states rather than heave in isolation. Deck travel bounds the motion that can be flown indoors, and taking the vision-estimated pipeline outdoors removes that bound and the cooperative marker together. The barrier covers the vertical channel under an assumed braking bound, and extending it to the lateral channels and to a bound identified in flight would widen the guarantee. Sec.

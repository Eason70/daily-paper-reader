# A Unified Dynamics Framework for Reinforcement Learning and Classical Control of a Six-DOF Pipeline-Tracking ROV in NVIDIA Isaac Sim 

Cheng Siong Chin, M. Venkateshkumar, and Jianhua Zhang 

Abstract —Reinforcement learning controllers for underwater vehicles are usually trained against one physics representation and, when a live simulator or hardware is available, deployed against another, so reported tracking performance does not always describe what the controller will do once it leaves the training environment. This paper reports a pipeline-tracking con-trol architecture for a six-degree-of-freedom remotely operated vehicle (ROV) in which a single Universal Scene Description (USD) scene supplies the real BlueROV2-Heavy mass, added-mass, damping, buoyancy, and eight-thruster allocation param-eters to both halves of the system: a vectorized NumPy imple-mentation of Fossen’s marine-craft equations, and an interactive NVIDIA Isaac Sim deployment in which the identical equations are applied as forces to a PhysX rigid body at every physics step. The Coriolis-centripetal coupling term C( ν)ν is included as the primary dynamics model in both branches, and a controlled ablation restricted to proximal policy optimization (PPO) and trust region policy optimization (TRPO) shows that including the term does not destabilize either algorithm and modestly improves tracking accuracy, an approximately 19 percent reduction in standoff root-mean-square error for TRPO. Five reinforcement learning algorithms, PPO, soft actor-critic (SAC), twin-delayed deep deterministic policy gradient (TD3), deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG), and TRPO, are trained against an identical environment, reward, and 30-episode randomized eval-uation harness through a checkpoint-compatibility layer that lets any trained policy be scored by the same evaluation code. The same shared-dynamics pipeline is extended with six classical and model-based control baselines, proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control, sliding-mode control, fuzzy logic control, feedback linearization, model predictive control, and an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS), each driven by the identical guidance geometry and thruster-allocation map as the learned policies, so that any performance difference reflects the control law itself. Under Coriolis-enabled dynamics, PPO, TRPO, and feedback linearization achieve the strongest combination of a 100 percent success rate and competitive-to-best tracking accuracy; PID, fuzzy logic control, and the ANFIS surrogate also reach 100 percent success with somewhat looser tracking; DDPG and TD3 each show a specific, explainable failure mode rather than a general weakness of off-policy learning; and classical, model-based control remains a strong, competitive, low-cost baseline against the best learned policies on this task.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Subsea pipelines carry oil, gas, and slurry across seabeds that divers and tethered inspection vehicles cannot reach cheaply or safely, and the standard inspection task for a remotely operated vehicle (ROV) is to fly a fixed standoff distance above the pipeline while avoiding rock outcrops and the seafloor itself [1]. The six coupled degrees of freedom of an underwater vehicle, surge, sway, heave, roll, pitch, and yaw, make the task difficult to hand-code, since damping, added mass, Coriolis-centripetal coupling, and buoyancy in-teract nonlinearly with vehicle attitude [2]. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an alternative to hand-tuned controllers: a policy trained against a physics model learns the coupling directly from reward signal rather than from a linearized approximation of it, and recent work reports reinforcement-learning controllers [3]–[5] that track paths, hold depth, and dock autonomous underwater vehicles under disturbances that defeat a fixed-gain proportional-integral-derivative loop. Most of this work is validated inside the same simulator used for training, with a single algorithm, and rarely against a classical or model-based controller built from the same dynamics model. A policy is trained and evaluated against one instance of a dynamics model, sometimes with domain randomization applied to the model’s own parameters, and the paper reports how that policy performed inside the training loop [6], [7]. Whether the same policy behaves identically once handed to a different physics engine, whether a reported result generalizes across the family of algorithms used for continuous control, and whether a learned policy actually outperforms a well-tuned classical controller given the same dynamics model and guidance geometry, are typically left open [8]. NVIDIA Isaac Sim narrows the first question for many robot classes by pairing a GPU-accelerated PhysX engine with a Universal Scene Description (USD) scene graph that serves as a single source of truth for a robot’s geometry and physical parameters [9], [10], and a recent underwater-specific extension, MarineGym, adapts Isaac Sim’s physics backbone to marine hydrodynamics at high simulation throughput [11]. A worked architecture in which the same, Coriolis-coupled equations of motion, read from one parameter set, drive a arXiv:2610.04949v1 [cs.RO] 4 Oct 2026 CHIN ET AL. : UNIFIED FOSSEN-DYNAMICS FRAMEWORK FOR RL AND CLASSICAL CONTROL OF A PIPELINE-TRACKING ROV 2

vectorized trainer, an interactive deployment scene, and a shared bank of classical baselines alike, has not been reported for a pipeline-tracking task. This paper builds a USD scene encoding a 6-DOF ROV’s mass, added mass, linear and quadratic damping, buoyancy, and eight-thruster allocation matrix as scene attributes, parsed by two independent code paths: a NumPy-vectorized imple-mentation of Fossen’s marine-craft dynamics [2], with the Coriolis-centripetal coupling term C( ν)ν included as part of the default governing equation, integrated with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge-Kutta scheme across 64 parallel environ-ments; and an interactive Isaac Sim deployment in which a controller reads the live PhysX pose and velocity of the rigid body at every physics step, computes the identical Coriolis, damping, thruster, and buoyancy terms, and applies them as a body-frame force and torque, letting PhysX integrate the vehicle in place of a second Runge-Kutta solver. A controlled ablation, restricted to two of the five compared reinforcement-learning algorithms, shows that including this term is safe and modestly beneficial, motivating its use as the primary model throughout rather than as a simplification to be en-gineered around. Against this Coriolis-inclusive architecture, five reinforcement learning algorithms are derived, trained, and evaluated: PPO [12] with generalized advantage estimation [13], SAC [14], TD3 [15], DDPG [16], and TRPO [17], scored through a single checkpoint-compatibility layer built around PPO’s own evaluation code; and six classical and model-based controllers, PID, sliding-mode control, fuzzy logic control, feedback linearization, model predictive control, and an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system, are added as baselines under the identical guidance geometry and thruster-allocation map, so that any performance difference reflects the control law rather than the task setup. Sections III through V present the shared architecture, dynamics, and RL envi-ronment; Sections VI and VII derive the five reinforcement-learning algorithms; Section VIII presents the six classical and model-based baselines; Section IX describes the Isaac Sim deployment; Sections X and XI give the evaluation protocol and the resulting comparison; Section XII discusses the comparison and its limitations. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Guidance and control for underwater vehicles 

Line-of-sight guidance coupled with proportional-integral-derivative or sliding-mode control remains the reference ap-proach for autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) path follow-ing; a recent survey documents three decades of refinements to the guidance law, from constant look-ahead distance to adaptive and time-varying variants that cope with current disturbances [1]. These methods are computationally cheap and well understood, but each new disturbance model or actuator constraint typically requires a new stability proof, and the resulting controllers do not adapt online to dynamics they were not designed against. 

B. Reinforcement learning for underwater tracking and fol-lowing 

Deep deterministic policy gradient and its twin-delayed variant have been used for AUV path following and depth tracking under current disturbance, with reported reductions in cross-track error of up to two-thirds relative to a PID baseline once a recurrent state encoder and a learnable guid-ance parameter are added [4]. Proximal policy optimization has been used for three-degree-of-freedom motion planning without a prior disturbance model [8], and soft actor-critic variants, sometimes combined with a long short-term memory encoder, produce adaptive trajectory-tracking controllers for underwater vehicles [18], [19]. Obstacle-aware extensions add a real-time avoidance term to the reward or the action space, closer to the pipeline-tracking task addressed here, since a policy that only tracks a centerline still routes around fixed and moving obstacles [20]. Across this literature, a learned policy generalizes across current strength and payload changes that would require re-derivation of a classical controller; the recurring gap is that a policy is validated inside the dynamics model it was trained against, with sim-to-real or sim-to-sim transfer left as future work [3], [5], that a single algorithm is typically reported per task, and that a classical baseline built from the same dynamics model is rarely reported alongside the learned policy. 

C. Off-policy and trust-region continuous control 

DDPG combines a deterministic policy with an actor-critic architecture and a replay buffer, extending value-based deep reinforcement learning to continuous action spaces, and is sensitive to function-approximation error in its single critic and to exploration-noise scale [16]. TD3 addresses this with twin critics under clipped double-Q learning, delayed policy updates, and target-policy smoothing noise, reporting more stable learning than DDPG on standard continuous-control benchmarks [15]. SAC frames the objective as maximum-entropy reinforcement learning, adding an entropy bonus to the reward and automatically tuning the temperature coef-ficient against a target entropy [14]. TRPO constrains each policy update to a trust region bounded by the Kullback-Leibler divergence between successive policies, computed via a conjugate-gradient approximation to the natural gradient and enforced with a backtracking line search, and is the on-policy predecessor PPO’s clipped surrogate objective was designed to approximate more cheaply [17]. Comparisons spanning all of these families against one environment, reward, and evaluation code are less common than single-algorithm reports, which motivates the comparison undertaken here. 

D. Simulation platforms and the sim-to-real gap 

Purpose-built underwater simulators such as HoloOcean and UNav-Sim provide photorealistic rendering and sensor models for AUV research but were not designed for the through-put population-based reinforcement learning needs [21], [22]. General-purpose GPU-accelerated simulators built for terres-trial and aerial robots, most visibly Isaac Gym and its succes-sor Isaac Sim, solved the throughput problem by vectorizing CHIN ET AL. : UNIFIED FOSSEN-DYNAMICS FRAMEWORK FOR RL AND CLASSICAL CONTROL OF A PIPELINE-TRACKING ROV 3

physics and observation computation on the GPU [9], [23]. Isaac Sim’s Universal Scene Description graph lets a single scene file describe a robot’s geometry, sensors, and physical parameters once, consumed by rendering, physics, and any downstream training or deployment script that opens the same file [10]. MarineGym extends Isaac Sim’s PhysX backbone with a hydrodynamic plugin for underwater vehicles and a domain-randomization toolkit aimed at narrowing the sim-to-real gap [11]. Domain randomization itself, varying physical parameters during training so a policy does not overfit to one simulator instance, was established for manipulation and has since been applied to marine vehicles directly [24], [25]. 

E. The Omniverse, Isaac Sim, and Isaac Lab simulation stack 

Three related but distinct layers of NVIDIA’s robotics-simulation stack (see Figure 1) are referred to throughout this paper and are worth separating explicitly. Omniverse is the underlying platform and engine: it provides the core 3D graphics, physics, and simulation infrastructure, together with the USD-based scene representation of Section III-A, on which higher-level tools are built. Isaac Sim is the robotics simulation environment built on Omniverse, supplying the virtual world, robots, sensors, physics, and robotics-specific tooling, including a ROS 2 bridge, needed to construct and test a vehicle such as the ROV modelled here. Isaac Lab is a further, reinforcement-learning-oriented framework built around Isaac Sim, providing a vectorized training environment and tooling for running many parallel simulation instances against a single policy-optimization loop [26]. An engine, in this sense, is the software infrastructure that performs the underlying computation, rendering, collision and force calculation, and standardized scene representation, so that applications built on top of it do not have to develop these capabilities from scratch, in the same sense that a car’s engine supplies motive power a driver does not directly interact with. A generic Isaac-centred workflow for this class of problem proceeds in four stages: building and validating a vehicle model and its hydrodynamic behaviour inside Isaac Sim’s GUI and visual debugging tools; defining a reinforcement-learning task in Isaac Lab by specifying observations (vehicle pose, velocity, and target waypoint), actions (thruster commands), and a reward (reaching the target while minimizing energy consumption); training a control policy with many parallel environment instances running under Isaac Lab; and deploying the trained policy back into Isaac Sim for visual evaluation, optionally bridged to ROS 2 for robotics-system integration. Figure 2 illustrates this pipeline in the general case, including an optional connection to a physical vehicle. The architecture reported in this paper (Section III onward) follows the Isaac Sim half of this stack directly: the ROV model and its hydrodynamics are built and validated as a USD scene, and the trained policy is deployed and evaluated visually inside Isaac Sim, with an equivalent ROS 2 bridge available but not exercised. S HARED -D YNAMICS SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 

A. The USD scene as the single parameter source 

The vehicle, the pipeline, the seafloor, and a set of random-izable spherical rock obstacles are described in one Universal Scene Description scene, and the ROV prim carries custom attributes alongside the standard PhysX rigid-body schema: rigid-body mass, an explicit weight and buoyancy pair, six diagonal added-mass coefficients, six linear and six quadratic damping coefficients, a maximum per-thruster force, an eight-thruster (6x8) allocation matrix, and the offset of the centre of buoyancy from the body origin. Every script in the system, whether it trains a reinforcement-learning policy, runs one of the six classical baselines of Section VIII, or flies a controller inside Isaac Sim, parses this same file at start-up. Gravity is disabled at the rigid-body level for the ROV prim, so the full weight-versus-buoyancy restoring force of Section IV-D is supplied explicitly by whichever controller is active, and does not have to be reasoned about separately for the training and deployment models. This architecture’s vehicle model uses the real BlueROV2-Heavy mass, added-mass, damping, and eight-thruster allocation matrix sourced from a vehicle-specific reference script (BlueRov m script.m). All five reinforcement-learning algorithms of Sections VI and VII, and all six classical baselines of Section VIII, are built and trained against the eight-actuator environment. 

B. Training and deployment as two consumers of one model 

Figure 1 sets out the two branches that read from the same simulation scene. The right-hand side shows the NVIDIA Isaac Sim 6.0.1 environment for ROV pipeline tracking, comprising a simulated ROV, an L-shaped pipeline, waypoint CHIN ET AL. : UNIFIED FOSSEN-DYNAMICS FRAMEWORK FOR RL AND CLASSICAL CONTROL OF A PIPELINE-TRACKING ROV 4

markers, and surrounding obstacles. The 3D scene provides a physics-based environment for visualising and evaluating ROV navigation and control. In contrast, the training branch uses a NumPy-vectorised Fossen dynamics engine to run 64 environments in parallel with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta integrator at a 0.05 s timestep, providing the basis for the five reinforcement-learning trainers in Sections VI and VII and the six classical controllers in Section VIII. The deployment branch loads the same scene in Isaac Sim, attaches a controller to the ROV prim, loads either a trained policy checkpoint or classical control law, generates thruster commands at a rate consistent with the training timestep, and applies the resulting forces to the PhysX rigid body at each physics step, with PhysX performing the integration. The two branches do not exchange state directly; only the trained policy checkpoint or tuned classical-controller parameters are transferred. Both branches implement the governing equation presented in Section IV-A using parameters from the same file, while the numerical differences arising from the integration method and the deployment branch’s algebraic treatment of added mass and the Coriolis–centripetal term are discussed in Section IX. B. Coriolis-centripetal matrix 

The Coriolis-centripetal matrix is the sum of a rigid-body term and an added-mass term, C( ν) = C RB( ν) + C A( ν), each built from the corresponding block of a mass matrix by the standard skew-symmetric cross-product construction used throughout the marine-craft literature [2]. Writing ν1 =[u,v,w] T and ν2 = [p,q,r] T for the linear and angular halves of the body-frame velocity, and M 11 , M 12 , M 21 , M 22 for the corresponding 3 ×3 blocks of a mass matrix M, the general skew-symmetric Coriolis-centripetal matrix associated with M takes the block form 

C(ν) = CRB (ν) + CA(ν) (5) with each 3 ×3 block of C( ν) built from the cross-product operator S(a)b = a × b applied to the linear-momentum-like vector M 11 ν1 + M 12 ν2 and the angular-momentum-like vector M 21 ν1 + M 22 ν2, so that the upper-right, lower-left, and lower-right 3 ×3 blocks of C( ν) are -S(M 11 ν1+M 12 ν2), -S(M 11 ν1+M 12 ν2), and -S(M 21 ν1+M 22 ν2) respectively, with the upper-left block identically zero [2]. The Fossen model of Section IV is wrapped in a vector-ized, Gym-style environment, PipelineTrackingEnv, running 64 independent vehicle instances in NumPy. The environ-ment, reward, and safety limits are identical across all five reinforcement-learning algorithms of Sections VI and VII, and the same guidance signals feed the six classical baselines of Section VIII. 

A. Episode length and control rate 

Episodes run for up to 1,040 steps of 0.05 s each (52 s), a budget set from the nominal transit time of the roughly 10 m L-shaped pipeline route with headroom for manoeuvring around its one corner and the randomized rocks. The policy acts at 20 Hz; inside the live Isaac Sim deployment of Section IX, physics runs at 60 Hz and the controller is queried once every third step. 

B. Observation

D. Termination and domain randomization 

Each episode terminates under one of six conditions, four of which count as failures. A collision is registered when the vehicle’s distance to any rock surface drops below its collision radius, or when the vehicle reaches the seafloor. A pipe-gap violation occurs once the distance to the centerline falls below the 1.0 m hard safety floor. A flip is declared when the absolute roll or pitch angle exceeds 75 degrees, and the episode also ends if the vehicle leaves the operating bounds. Success is recorded when the vehicle comes within the 0.30 m waypoint radius of the route’s final point. Any episode that reaches the maximum length without meeting one of these conditions ends in timeout. At every reset, the horizontal position and radius of each rock are jittered within a narrow band, so the policy is never trained against an identical obstacle layout twice. 

E. A corner-cutting projection exploit 

The pipeline geometry includes a single 90-degree turn, and along-track progress is credited by projecting the vehicle’s position onto the nearest point of a piecewise-linear centerline, one segment at a time. With that projection computed indepen-dently per segment, a diagonal path can project favorably onto the far segment of the corner without the vehicle approaching the true corner vertex, collecting progress reward for a route it never flew; one diagnosed episode reached 9.31 m of a 10.00 m route while never coming closer than 1.86 m to the vertex. A per-environment projection gate, requiring the vehicle to close within a fixed radius of a segment’s end vertex before projection onto the next segment is permitted, was verified against a synthetic corner-cutting path (rejected, growing tracked error) and a genuine cornering path (unlocked and tracked correctly). This projection exploit addresses how progress reward is credited by the guidance geometry itself, independently of any particular algorithm’s or controller’s own training or tuning dynamics, and the fix, together with the look-ahead heading point of Section VIII’s closing paragraph, benefits every reinforcement-learning and classical controller compared in this paper equally. A. Design considerations for replay-buffer training 

Domain-randomized rock positions work with a replay buffer because the distance and bearing to the nearest rock are fully observed. The curriculum schedule, which trains first on a short scene and then on the full-length scene, causes a problem for off-policy methods. Total pipeline length differs between the two phases and is not part of the observation. The same observed along-track distance can therefore correspond to a different true reward depending on which phase generated it, and a persistent buffer holding transitions from both phases would alias them together. For this reason, the curriculum is disabled for SAC, TD3, and DDPG. TRPO is on-policy, so it retains the curriculum. To manage the sensitivity of SAC, TD3, and DDPG to reward magnitude, a global multiplier of reward scale = 0.1 multiplier is applied only to the critic’s temporal-difference target, leaving the reward’s own gains unchanged. BASELINES 

To place the five learned policies of Sections VI and VII alongside the standard tools of marine control engineering, six classical and model-based controllers are implemented on the identical PipelineTrackingEnv guidance signals and thruster allocation of Section V. They are evaluated by exactly the same code as the five reinforcement-learning policies (Section X). None of the six baselines is trained by reinforcement learning. Five are hand-derived or hand-tuned control laws, and the sixth, the adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system of Section VIII-F, is fit by supervised learning to imitate one of the other five. All six share the guidance geometry, channel definitions, and thruster-allocation map summarized in Table IX and detailed in the closing paragraph of this section. A. Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) Control 

A standard per-channel proportional-integral-derivative (PID) loop runs directly on the same guidance-law errors that the reinforcement-learning policies observe. These are the horizontal and vertical cross-track/standoff errors, the look-ahead heading error described in the closing paragraph of this section, and the vehicle’s roll and pitch angles, which an active leveling term drives toward zero. Each of the six channels (surge, sway, heave, roll, pitch, and yaw) has its own independently tuned proportional, integral, and derivative gains. D. Feedback Linearization (FL) 

Feedback linearization is a model-based computed-torque controller [28]. It has privileged access to the same Fos-sen dynamics model that the simulator uses to propagate the vehicle, including the mass matrix, Coriolis-centripetal coupling (Section IV-B), damping, and restoring forces. At every control step, the controller evaluates these nonlinear terms at the vehicle’s current state and commands a wrench that cancels them exactly. The closed loop then reduces to a linear, decoupled error-dynamics system, driven by a desired acceleration that simple proportional-derivative gains set from the guidance-law error. Because the nonlinear dynamics are cancelled algebraically and not approximated, the outer PD gains can be tuned independently on each channel, free of the cross-coupling that complicates gain tuning for classical PID and SMC. By construction, feedback linearization is the cleanest and fastest of the six baselines to tune, since it needs no boundary-layer, membership-function, or rule-base design. Its cost is a dependence on an accurate dynamics model, which purely error-driven controllers such as PID and SMC do not need. A per-channel desired acceleration is formed from the guidance error exactly as for PID, but without an integral term, since the model cancellation below removes the steady-state bias an integrator would otherwise be needed to correct: 

a = kpe + kd ˙e (50) and the commanded wrench cancels the governing equa-tion’s nonlinear terms (Equation 1) about the vehicle’s current state while imposing this desired acceleration on the mass matrix:

E. Model Predictive Control (MPC) 

The model predictive controller (MPC) replans a 20-step action sequence at every control step using the cross-entropy method (CEM) [31]. A population of candidate sequences is rolled out through the same Fossen model used for feedback linearization, and each candidate is scored by a quadratic tracking-and-effort cost over the horizon. Over two CEM iterations, a Gaussian action distribution is refit to the best-scoring elite fraction of the previous iteration’s samples before the population is resampled. A common practice is to seed the first CEM iteration from the previous step’s plan. Here, each replanning step is instead warm-started from a fresh feedback-linearization trajectory (Section VIII-D) computed over the same horizon, which gives the sampler a strong, dynamically consistent starting point in place of an open-loop guess. In standard receding-horizon fashion, only the first action of the resulting plan is applied before the full horizon is replanned. The executed action, however, departs from the usual choice of the elite-mean sequence. IX. D EPLOYMENT INSIDE NVIDIA I SAAC SIM 

Once training has produced a checkpoint, or a classical controller’s gains have been tuned, the same USD scene is opened inside Isaac Sim and a controller script is attached to the vehicle prim. At every physics step, the script reads the current world pose and rotation matrix from the rigid body and rotates the world-frame linear and angular velocities into the body frame to obtain ν. From ν, it computes the added-mass Coriolis-centripetal correction (Section IX-B) and the damping (Section IV-C). The thruster wrench follows from the current thrust command and the allocation matrix (Section IV-E). Because PhysX gravity is disabled for this body, the restoring force (Section IV-D) is also computed in the script and rotated into the body frame. The Coriolis correction, damping, thrust, and buoyancy terms are then summed into a single body-frame force and torque. PhysX integrates the resulting motion, so no second Runge-Kutta solver runs alongside it.

B. The Coriolis-centripetal term in the live controller 

The Coriolis-centripetal term C( ν)ν of Section IV-B is included in both the training-side Fossen model and the live Isaac Sim controller, but is not applied identically in the two branches, because the two branches integrate motion by different means. The training-side Runge-Kutta integrator has no notion of rigid-body dynamics beyond the explicit right-hand side supplied to it, so the full term, C RB( ν)ν plus C A( ν)ν, is computed and applied there. In the live Isaac Sim controller, only the added-mass Coriolis correction C A( ν)ν

is applied explicitly, because PhysX’s own rigid-body solver already supplies the rigid-body Coriolis term C RB( ν)ν in-trinsically as part of its native integration of a rotating rigid body; applying it a second time, on top of PhysX’s own treatment of rigid-body rotation, would double-count it and inject a spurious torque with no physical counterpart. The rigid-body part of the Coriolis effect must be counted exactly once. The training-side model applies it directly, while in live deployment PhysX already accounts for it as it moves the vehicle, so the Isaac Sim controller adds only the added-mass part. Both setups therefore run on the same equations of motion. The ablation in Section XI-A, carried out on the training-side model with both Coriolis components applied explicitly, is what shows that including the full Coriolis term is safe for PPO and TRPO. The deployment split described here simply ensures that the model tested in that ablation matches the one that actually drives the vehicle in Isaac Sim. It is a detail of how the motion is integrated and makes no separate claim of its own. X. E VALUATION PROTOCOL

A. Coriolis-term ablation (PPO and TRPO) 

Before the full set of controllers is compared, the Coriolis-centripetal term introduced in Section IV-B is validated through a controlled ablation restricted to PPO and TRPO (Fig. 5). Each controller is evaluated over 30 episodes. In every episode, the ROV starts at the beginning of the pipeline and attempts to follow it to the end. An episode counts as a success only if the vehicle reaches within 0.30 m of the route’s final point before any failure occurs. It counts as a failure if the vehicle collides with a rock or the seafloor, comes closer than 1.0 m to the pipe, flips, leaves the permitted operating area, or runs out of time. Each algorithm was trained twice, once with C( ν)ν included in the Fossen model and once with it removed, while every other environment, reward, and training setting was held fixed. The four resulting checkpoints were then scored with the identical 30-episode randomized evaluation protocol of Section X. TD3, DDPG, and SAC were also examined informally while this ablation was developed, but their results are not reported numerically here. The comparison is intended as a focused before-and-after test on the two algorithms for which the released Isaac Sim controller of Section IX-B applies the term, and results from three further algorithms would not sharpen that specific comparison. With the Coriolis term included, both algorithms keep a 100 percent success rate with zero flips and zero corridor-gap violations, and both track more accurately. PPO’s standoff RMSE falls from 0.152 m to 0.146 m. TRPO’s falls from 0.176 m to 0.142 m, a reduction of approximately 19 percent. Adding Coriolis-centripetal coupling therefore leaves both algorithms stable and improves their tracking. On this basis, the full coupling is treated as the primary dynamics model for the rest of this paper, with no simplification needed to work around it.

C. Other findings 

The corner-cutting projection exploit found during environ-ment development (Section V-E) comes from the guidance ge-ometry and affects every controller alike. An early, unguarded projection of the vehicle’s position onto the piecewise-linear pipeline centerline allowed a diagonal path to collect progress reward by cutting across the outside of the route’s corner without ever approaching the true corner vertex. Two fixes close this gap: the per-segment projection gate of Section V-E and the look-ahead heading point described in the closing paragraph of Section VIII. Every controller compared in Table XIX benefits from both equally. DDPG’s 30 percent flip rate in Table XIX is consistent with its action-magnitude value of 0.758, roughly two to three times the 0.230 to 0.448 range of every other controller in the table. A policy that commands much larger thruster forces on average is also the one most prone to the large, sudden attitude changes that end in a flip. B. Reinforcement learning versus classical and model-based control 

Table XIX’s main comparison shows that model-based feedback linearization and the two on-policy/trust-region reinforcement-learning policies, PPO and TRPO, achieve the strongest combination of a 100 percent success rate and competitive-to-best tracking accuracy, 0.146 m, 0.142 m, and 0.231 m RMSE respectively. PID, fuzzy logic control, and ANFIS all also reach a 100 percent success rate but with worse tracking accuracy, in roughly that order, PID best of the three at 0.215 m, ANFIS close behind at 0.211 m, and fuzzy logic control at 0.243 m; sliding-mode control reaches 100 percent success but the worst tracking accuracy of any successful controller, 0.293 m, consistent with the boundary-layer reaching law of Section VIII-B trading tracking precision for smooth, chatter-free thruster commands. Model predictive control trades some success rate, 80 percent, for RMSE, 0.177 m, competitive with PID and close to PPO and TRPO; a shooting-based planner limited to a 20-step horizon and two cross-entropy-method iterations (Section VIII-E) can produce an occasional short-sighted or locally infeasible plan around the route’s single corner without enough horizon or refinement to recover from it within an episode, plausibly the source of MPC’s non-zero corridor-gap-violation rate, the only classical controller with one. Among the reinforcement-learning policies, PPO and TRPO clearly dominate DDPG and TD3 under this reward and training budget. DDPG’s instability, a 30 percent flip rate and an action magnitude roughly two to three times every other controller’s (Section XI-C), is consistent with the known brittleness of a single-critic deterministic policy gradient under Q-overestimation; TD3’s corner-parking (Section XI-B) is a specific, reproducible degenerate local optimum tied to this checkpoint’s exploration-noise and discount-factor setup, not evidence that off-policy methods generally fail this task, since TD3 tracks the portion of the route it does traverse more tightly than any other controller in the comparison. The broader synthesis is that classical and model-based control, with no learning involved at all for PID and feedback linearization, and comparatively little for the hand-designed fuzzy controller, remains highly competitive with the best reinforcement-learning policies on this task. Feedback lin-earization in particular, which has privileged access to the true dynamics model, matches or exceeds the tracking accuracy of PPO and TRPO without any training cost at all, an honest and important caveat to any narrative in which reinforcement learning is assumed to outperform classical control outright. Reinforcement learning’s advantage here is less about outright dominance on this metric set and more about not requiring a hand-derived dynamics model, a gain-tuning process, or a rule base, at the cost of sensitivity to algorithm choice and training budget, the specific TD3 and DDPG failure modes above being the clearest illustration of that cost. Lastly, the MPC baseline uses a deliberately lightweight planning budget (Section VIII-E), so its 80 percent success rate may understate what shooting-based MPC can achieve. The ANFIS baseline (Section VIII-F) is a learned clone of PID and was not optimized independently. All experiments use a single L-shaped route of roughly 10 m with narrow rock randomization, and each controller comes from a sin-gle run. Both the Coriolis-term ablation and the classical-control comparison show the value of testing a modelling or algorithm choice through controlled comparison on the same evaluation harness. Neither the safety of a simplification nor the advantage of a learned policy over a hand-derived one should be assumed in advance. XIII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented a pipeline-tracking control architecture for a six-degree-of-freedom ROV, in which a single USD scene supplies Coriolis-coupled Fossen dynamics parameters to both a vectorized training model and an interactive NVIDIA Isaac Sim deployment. A controlled ablation over PPO and TRPO validated the inclusion of the Coriolis-centripetal term. Including the Coriolis term leaves PPO and TRPO stable and improves their tracking accuracy, most clearly for TRPO, which supports modelling it by default for this task family. In the live Isaac Sim controller, only the added-mass component is applied explicitly, since the PhysX solver already supplies the rigid-body component.

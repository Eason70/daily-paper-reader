Keywords: Differential Games, Model Predictive Control, Feedback Nash Equilibrium, Constrained Games, Multi-Agent Control 

Physical human–machine interaction and other multi-agent control settings require decision-making policies that adapt online. Differential games provide a principled framework where each agent optimizes an individual objective while anticipating the other’s response. The relevant solution concept in many applications is the feedback Nash equilibrium (FNE), which yields time-consistent state-feedback strategies. However, computing the FNE is demanding and becomes intractable when state and input constraints must be enforced, motivating the need for approximate methods. This paper presents a Model Predictive Control (MPC) approach that approximates infinite-horizon FNE trajectories through repeated solution of finite-horizon open-loop games. An auxiliary-game formulation is introduced that selects prediction horizons and terminal costs to approximate the feedback-game optimality conditions. The approach is extended to incorporate hard constraints via a constrained open-loop game formulation. For the unconstrained setting, an analytic upper bound on the state-trajectory deviation between the MPC-induced and FNE trajectories is derived, enabling quantitative performance certification. Numerical examples illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed method compared with baseline approaches from the literature. 

## 1 Introduction 

Physical human–machine interaction (pHMI) and shared-control systems require controllers that adapt online to a partner’s actions while enforcing safety constraints and actuator limits. In driving-assistance and haptic guidance, a common design goal is to continuously negotiate control authority and intent through the shared interface [1, 2, 3]. These applications involve real-time interaction between human and machine, where both agents continuously adjust their strategies based on the evolving state of the interaction. This iterative shared control paradigm, often implemented through haptic feedback, demands control algorithms that can respond to changing circumstances within tight computational budgets. Game-theoretic formulations are a well-established fit for these settings because they explicitly model strategic coupling between agents and the resulting closed-loop interaction [4, 5]. The application of differential games to human–machine interaction is particularly well-suited because it treats both agents (human and machine) as players in a noncooperative game. Each player optimizes their own objective while anticipating the other player’s response, which naturally captures the interaction in shared control settings. Recent work on human-in-the-loop shared control emphasizes the importance of online adaptation while maintaining safety guarantees [6, 7]. In many interactive control problems, the most appropriate equilibrium concept is a feedback Nash equilibrium (FNE) because it produces time-consistent state-feedback policies. However, computing FNEs for general nonlinear differential games is rarely tractable in real time, as it involves solving coupled Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations [8]. Even in the linear–quadratic (LQ) case, feedback and open-loop equilibria can differ substantially, so repeatedly applying open-loop solutions may yield 

1arXiv:2610.04614v1 [eess.SY] 3 Oct 2026 systematic closed-loop mismatch [9]. Existing numerical game solvers can compute local equilibria in nonlinear settings and can incorporate constraints, but their computational cost is often incompatible with the sampling rates required in pHMI and shared-control applications [10, 11]. Model predictive control (MPC) is the standard framework for online optimization with explicit con-straint handling via repeated finite-horizon re-optimization [12, 13]. This motivates moving-horizon and game-theoretic MPC schemes that repeatedly solve finite-horizon local games [14, 15, 16, 17]. Neverthe-less, solving finite-horizon open-loop games inside MPC in general fails to recover the infinite-horizon FNE of the underlying interaction, and the resulting gap resists a priori quantification. This paper addresses approximate computation of FNE behavior in constrained differential games using an MPC-based construction that preserves standard feasibility machinery while explicitly targeting feedback-game optimality. The main contributions are: • an auxiliary-game MPC formulation that selects the prediction horizon and terminal costs to reduce the mismatch to the infinite-horizon feedback-game optimality conditions, including coupling terms between players; • an extension to hard state and input constraints via constrained open-loop local-game solves; and • for the unconstrained setting, an analytic upper bound on the deviation between the MPC-induced trajectory and the infinite-horizon FNE trajectory, enabling quantitative certification of approxi-mation quality. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work; Section 3 sum-marizes differential-game preliminaries; Section 4 presents the auxiliary-game MPC approximation for constrained games; Section 5 derives upper bounds for the unconstrained trajectory deviation; and Section 6 reports numerical results. 

## 2 Related Work 

Differential games are commonly used to represent strategic coupling between agents (e.g., human and machine) in real-time interactive control. Across the literature, methods typically trade off among (i) the equilibrium solution concept (feedback Nash, open-loop Nash, or pragmatic non-equilibrium concepts), (ii) the computational machinery used (PDE/Riccati, iterative optimal control, learning), and (iii) their ability to explicitly enforce hard state/input constraints online. 

2.1 Exact Feedback Nash Equilibria 

For general nonlinear differential games, an exact Feedback Nash Equilibrium (FNE) is characterized by a coupled system of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equations. Solving these coupled PDEs is rarely feasible beyond low-dimensional settings due to the curse of dimensionality [8]. In the unconstrained linear–quadratic (LQ) case, the FNE can be computed via coupled Riccati equations, for which special-ized numerical algorithms and existence/uniqueness results are available [18]. However, for applications involving hard actuation limits and safety constraints (as in pHMI and shared control), Riccati-based solutions do not directly apply, motivating approximate or numerically tractable alternatives. 

2.2 Learning-Based Approximations of Feedback Structure 

A wide range of approaches use approximate dynamic programming and reinforcement learning to reduce the computational burden of coupled HJB equations while preserving feedback dependence. Within the LQ domain, policy-iteration schemes have been proposed that replace the coupled HJB system with iterative solutions of Lyapunov-like equations [19], and off-policy learning approaches have been shown

to recover FNE solutions under unknown dynamics assumptions [20]. For nonlinear games, value-function approximation (e.g., neural parameterizations) and actor–critic architectures are commonly used to approximate equilibrium strategies [21, 22]. A recurring limitation for safety-critical settings is that constraints are frequently handled indirectly (e.g., via penalties or saturation), which complicates hard feasibility guarantees [23, 24]. 

2.3 Constrained Receding-Horizon Game Formulations 

Hard constraints are best addressed in a receding-horizon framework, where MPC provides standard machinery for online optimization with explicit feasibility considerations [12]. This has led to Game-MPC formulations in which agents repeatedly solve finite-horizon dynamic games and apply the resulting actions in a receding-horizon manner [25]. Modern iterative solvers (e.g., based on sequential quadratic approximations) enable online computation of local equilibria in nonlinear settings [11, 10, 26, 27]. A central methodological gap is that many tractable online schemes compute finite-horizon open-loop 

Nash equilibria (OLNE). Because open-loop and feedback equilibria can differ substantially, repeated OLNE computation does not, in general, recover the infinite-horizon FNE [9]. Recent work has proposed intermediate equilibrium concepts and problem reformulations aimed at narrowing this discrepancy, including moving-horizon equilibrium notions and variational-inequality viewpoints [28, 29]. In contrast, the approach taken here preserves standard open-loop MPC machinery for constraint handling while introducing an auxiliary-game construction to approximate the infinite-horizon FNE and to provide an analytical bound on the induced trajectory deviation. 

2.4 Research Gap and Contributions 

What is missing in the literature is a receding-horizon game formulation that keeps the constraint-handling of open-loop MPC while explicitly targeting, and approximating, the infinite-horizon feedback Nash equilibrium. Therefore, this paper develops an MPC-based method that approximates infinite-horizon FNE trajectories using repeated solutions of finite-horizon open-loop games. The key contribu-tions are as follows: (i) we introduce an auxiliary-game MPC formulation that selects horizon/terminal design to better match feedback-game optimality conditions, (ii) extend the approach to incorporate state and input constraints via constrained open-loop game solves, and (iii) provide an analytic upper bound on the state-trajectory deviation between the MPC-induced closed loop and the feedback Nash equilibrium in the unconstrained case.

## 4 Approximation of Feedback Nash Equilibria via Constrained MPC 

This section presents the main results of our work: a novel approach for approximating infinite-horizon FNE in LQ differential games using a constrained MPC framework. We consider infinite-horizon linear–quadratic (LQ) differential games with feedback information struc-ture and seek computationally tractable approximations of the associated feedback Nash equilibrium (FNE). The approach is based on a receding-horizon (MPC) construction: at each time, a finite-horizon 

open-loop game is solved and only the first control action is applied. This yields a state-feedback law through repeated re-optimization. While the presentation below focuses on two players, the formulation extends to N players by augmenting the dynamics with additional inputs and adding one cost functional per player; see, e.g., Chapters 7–8 in [30]. Section 4.1 summarizes the baseline unconstrained moving-horizon game formulation of [14] and high-lights the resulting feedback law, and Section 4.2 recalls its closed-form analytical solution. Section 4.3 introduces the auxiliary-game extension used in this work, in which local auxiliary games include termi-nal penalties to better approximate the infinite-horizon FNE, and Section 4.4 extends this construction to state and input constraints to approximate constrained infinite-horizon FNE trajectories. 

4.1 Model Predictive Control in Dynamic Games 

Model predictive control (MPC) constructs a feedback policy by repeatedly solving a finite-horizon optimization problem and applying only the initial control move. In the dynamic-game setting of [14], the finite-horizon problem is an open-loop game; in the continuous-time limit treated in this section, this local game is re-solved at every instant t and only the instantaneous control value is applied, so that the resulting receding-horizon implementation is a state-feedback policy with static gain KM P C , even though each local problem uses open-loop information. Throughout, we refer to the approach of [14] as the unconstrained MPC approach .Consider an LQ differential game over an infinite time horizon with feedback information structure. In the MPC context, this game is referred to as the global game .

4.3 Approximation of Feedback Nash Equilibria with an Auxiliary Game and Terminal Cost 

The baseline approach of [14] is analytically tractable in the unconstrained case and proposes selecting 

T to reduce the mismatch between the MPC-induced feedback and the global-game feedback optimality conditions. However, in multidimensional settings and in the presence of coupling terms in the cost, optimizing only the horizon length can be too restrictive, as demonstrated in Section 6. In this section, we consider an extension that augments each local game with terminal penalties and explicitly incorporates cross terms Rij in the performance index. Terminal penalties are standard in MPC and have also been analyzed in differential-game MPC variants [29], where they serve their classical role as a stability and feasibility ingredient for the receding-horizon open-loop equilibrium. Here, in contrast, Q1T and Q2T are free design parameters tuned jointly with the horizon T to match the optimality conditions of the infinite-horizon feedback game, which, together with explicit state and input constraints, is to the best of our knowledge not treated systematically in existing work. The resulting method introduces additional degrees of freedom beyond the horizon length and yields a constrained MPC scheme intended to approximate constrained FNE trajectories via repeated solution of constrained open-loop local games. ## 5 Upper Bounds for the Trajectory Error Between FNE and MPC Solu-tions 

In the previous section, a method for approximating FNE based on an MPC approach was introduced. It remains an open question whether the maximum error between state trajectories can be estimated using only the approximated solution and the reference solution from the coupled Riccati equation. This approach would eliminate the need to perform a simulation-based, a posteriori analysis for every individual scenario. The following section aims at deriving an upper bound for the trajectory error estimates of the MPC and FNE solution for the unconstrained case. The literature on trajectory-error estimation in differential games highlights three relevant directions: Lyapunov-based bounds, matrix-exponential norm estimates, and ε-Nash concepts. A dynamic neural-network identification method with Lyapunov analysis for N -player nonzero-sum games with unknown nonlinear dynamics is developed in [34], obtaining bounds primarily on input trajectory errors. In [35], extremum seeking and Lyapunov arguments are used to ensure bounded input deviations, but explicit bounds for state errors are not provided. In [36], ε-Nash equilibria are analyzed, which guarantee that no player can improve their cost by more than ε through unilateral deviation, but this does not directly translate to explicit state trajectory error bounds. In contrast, in [37], an explicit upper bound on state-trajectory deviations is derived via a norm-based distance metric between games, which is therefore most aligned with estimating maximum state error in our setting. Following [37], we use matrix-exponential bounds to estimate trajectory deviations in infinite-horizon LQ feedback games. ## 7 Conclusion and Outlook 

This paper presented an MPC-based method for approximating infinite-horizon feedback Nash equilibria in LQ differential games. The approach extends the baseline moving-horizon game formulation of [14] by introducing terminal cost matrices as additional optimization variables in each local open-loop game, thereby increasing the degrees of freedom available to reduce the mismatch with the global feedback-game optimality conditions. Cross-coupling terms between players were explicitly incorporated into the residual minimization, addressing a limitation of prior work that considered only the prediction horizon as a tunable parameter. The formulation was further extended to accommodate state and input constraints by embedding the auxiliary-game structure within a constrained MPC framework, where each local game is solved as a constrained open-loop Nash problem. For the unconstrained setting, an analytic upper bound on the state-trajectory deviation between the MPC-induced closed loop and the exact FNE was derived using matrix exponential norm estimates. The bound depends on the feedback gain mismatch, the stability margins of both closed-loop systems, and the initial condition, and was shown to be practically informative in the numerical examples considered. Future work will focus on three directions: (i) numerical validation of the constrained formulation on benchmark systems relevant to shared control and physical human–machine interaction, see [39], (ii) investigation of tighter matrix exponential bounds to improve the conservatism of the error estimate, and (iii) extension of the approach to nonlinear differential games via iterative linearization within the receding-horizon loop.

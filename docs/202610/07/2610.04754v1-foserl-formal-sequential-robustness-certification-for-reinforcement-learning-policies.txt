Abstract 

Even a few action perturbations can substantially degrade the performance of a deployed decision policy. Cer-tifying the resulting return loss is challenging in stochastic environments, where returns vary even without an attack. We introduce FoSeRL, a framework for certifying deployed RL policies against precommitted, tempo-rally sparse action attacks. The deployed policy is unchanged, with no smoothing or retraining. Certification requires a resettable simulator supporting shared randomness and independent one-step successor queries, but no analytical dynamics model. FoSeRL certifies that an attacked episode loses no more than a prescribed amount of return relative to the same episode unattacked, with at least a target probability and at a user-specified con-fidence level. Both runs share the initial state and randomness, so the measured loss reflects the attack, not the episode; carrying the running return gap as a state coordinate makes it the terminal value, reducing trajectory-level certification to terminal safety. Time-dependent barrier conditions on the augmented state bound the terminal failure probability: satisfied exactly, they certify every admissible precommitted attack; learned from sampled trajectories and verified on held-out data, they certify the same guarantee under a specified attack-episode setting. Across six stochastic continuous-control environments and three RL policy families (TD3, SAC, and PPO), FoSeRL certifies non-trivial cardinality–magnitude robustness frontiers, achieves substantially larger certified budgets than policy smoothing, and reveals marked robustness differences among policies with comparable nominal performance. 

## 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning (RL) policies have demonstrated control of physical systems, including tokamak plasmas (Degrave et al., 2022), stratospheric balloons (Bellemare et al., 2020), and racing drones (Kaufmann et al., 2023). Once such a policy is deployed, its decisions do not reach the plant directly: each command travels through communication links and actuation software before an actuator executes it. Every stage of that path is a place where an adversary with modest resources can intervene, as end-to-end attacks on production automotive control systems have shown (Koscher et al., 2010; Checkoway et al., 2011). The executed action can therefore differ from the command computed by the policy, even though the deployed policy itself remains unchanged. Thus, we study deployment-time perturbations of executed actions, with the decision policy held fixed. The effect of such perturbations depends on their magnitude and, just as much, on their timing. On five Atari games, Lin et al. (2017) found that perturbing roughly a quarter of the time steps reduced return about as much as perturbing every step. The reason is that the closed loop is a dynamical system: a perturbed action changes the state from which all later decisions are made, so its effect persists after the perturbation itself has ended. An adversary with a limited budget can therefore concentrate it where the loop is most sensitive instead of spreading it uniformly, and the same holds when the perturbation is injected into the executed action rather than the observation (Lee et al., 2020). Two quantities govern what such an adversary can do: the number of perturbed time steps, 𝐾 , and a bound 𝑏 on the size of each perturbation. Certifying a deployed policy therefore means controlling their cumulative effect on return. In a stochastic environment, the nominal return already varies from episode to episode, so the baseline against which loss is measured becomes part of the certification problem. Comparing an attacked run to the policy’s average return would charge that variation to the adversary. We therefore compare each attacked execution with the run the same episode would have produced unattacked, using the same initial state and the same environment randomness, and measure the loss between the two. The attack is then the only difference between them, while the randomness that shapes how an attack unfolds is preserved rather than averaged away. Our objective is to identify the attack budgets under which this loss stays within a prescribed tolerance with a target probability.

Contributions. We introduce FoSeRL, a post-hoc framework for cer-tifying fixed deployed policies against temporally sparse action pertur-bations. Our contributions are threefold. (i) We formulate sequential ro-bustness under joint cardinality–magnitude budgets and define a paired return-degradation criterion using synchronously coupled nominal and attacked executions, isolating attack-induced loss from episode variabil-ity. (ii) We lift the cumulative return gap to a terminal quantity of an augmented stochastic system and derive time-dependent barrier condi-tions for its tail reliability (Fig. 1). (iii) We learn neural barrier certifi-cates from sampled rollouts and verify them using finite-sample suc-cessor bounds and episode-level conformal calibration, yielding high-confidence operational certificates under a declared attack–episode law. FoSeRL leaves the deployed policy unchanged and requires no analyti-cal dynamics model. Across six stochastic continuous-control environ-ments and TD3, SAC, and PPO policies, it yields non-trivial robustness frontiers and substantially larger certified budgets than the baseline. 

Related work. Temporally sparse attacks are well established empirically (Lin et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2020), including on the action channel (Lee et al., 2020), and defences against them harden a policy by retraining (Pinto et al., 2017; Tessler et al., 2019; Liang et al., 2022) without emitting a certificate. Certified robustness for RL does emit one, but of a different shape. Smoothing-based methods certify actions or cumulative reward under norm-bounded perturbations (Kumar et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022a; Mu et al., 2024); certified state-action bounds support robust action selection (Everett et al., 2021); and CAROL learns a policy jointly with a model-based robustness cer-tificate (Yang et al., 2024). Each bounds perturbations under a norm ball, typically guarantees an expected return, and certifies a smoothed, retrained, or robust-argmax surrogate rather than the policy as deployed. Stochastic and neural barrier certificates supply the machinery we build on (Prajna et al., 2007; Steinhardt and Tedrake, 2012; Abate et al., 2021; Anand and Zamani, 2023; Luo and Ma, 2021), but bound state-safety events rather than adversarial performance loss; the framework closest to ours certifies a supervised learner against data poisoning by modeling training as a stochastic dynamical system (Taheri and Zamani, 2026), and has no time axis for an attacker to exploit. Attacks in the literature are budgeted along two axes: how often the adversary strikes and how hard, but existing certificates fix one or the other: they either bound perturbations at every step or count corruptions without bounding their size. The cardinality–magnitude trade-off therefore appears throughout the 

attack literature and in no prior certificate . FoSeRL is the first to certify it, and the first to do so for the policy exactly as deployed, against perturbations of its executed actions, as a tail bound on paired degradation. Table 4 in Appendix F places each method on these axes.

86 Discussion 

FoSeRL provides post-hoc guarantees for the cumulative performance loss of an unchanged policy under tempo-rally sparse action attacks. Its paired-state construction makes return degradation a terminal quantity, connect-ing sequential robustness to stochastic barrier analysis and independent finite-sample verification. Experiments across six continuous-control environments and three RL policy families demonstrate non-trivial cardinality– magnitude robustness profiles and larger certified budgets than the evaluated smoothing baseline at the reported settings. 

Robustness at the episode level. The joint budget (𝐾, 𝑏 ) distinguishes how many actions can be perturbed from the strength of each perturbation, while cumulative degradation captures consequences that persist after the attack ends. Pairing each attacked execution with its nominal counterpart under shared initial conditions and random inputs provides a consistent reference for this loss. It avoids variability introduced by an independently randomized comparison without removing stochastic variation in the attack’s consequences, and gives exactly zero degradation when no attack occurs. The resulting tail criterion addresses excessive episode-level loss, rather than only average performance or instantaneous action changes. Coupling is therefore part of the robustness specification itself, not merely a device for improving estimation. 

Policy- and environment-agnostic certification. FoSeRL separates certification from how the policy is ob-tained. Under the stated execution and sampling assumptions, its argument does not depend on a particular RL algorithm, policy architecture, policy differentiability, or analytical family of dynamics. Learned and analytically specified policies, including deployment-time randomization, fit the same formulation. Relative to the robust-training, policy-smoothing, and model-based approaches reviewed in Appendix F, its distinguishing feature is that certification requires neither a robustness-specific policy modification nor a separately learned dynamics surrogate. Instead, coupled rollouts and fresh conditional successors support certification of the policy as de-ployed . This makes the framework applicable when the original training pipeline is unavailable and complemen-tary to robust policy learning: a policy can be assessed regardless of whether robustness was considered during its training. 

The value of a dynamical certificate. Direct terminal testing and FoSeRL address the same operational event but provide different certifying objects. A one-sided binomial bound on independent paired episodes can certify the terminal probability without barrier synthesis or successor queries. FoSeRL additionally provides a state– time witness linking initial conditions, expected one-step evolution, and terminal separation. Its residuals and reliability decomposition identify which certificate conditions and sampled episode phases limit witness quality, supporting targeted refinement. These are diagnostics, not certified conditional failure probabilities at arbitrary states. Direct testing can yield tighter budgets at lower cost by bypassing the sufficient barrier conditions. The trade-off is therefore scalar tightness and computation versus dynamical structure, not formal versus empirical validity. Appendix E.4 develops this distinction. 

Formal assurance from finite samples. Separating synthesis from verification allows flexible neural candi-date construction while grounding validity in an independent statistical test. Successor approximation uncer-tainty and out-of-sample violations are incorporated into the end-to-end guarantee, rather than inferred from training loss or empirical success rates. Episode-level calibration accommodates temporal dependence within each trajectory, and the empirical-to-exact transfer avoids an additional union-bound penalty over time. Uni-form satisfaction of the ideal barrier conditions covers every admissible precommitted attack; the data-driven procedure certifies the declared attack–episode law (Theorem 2). This is a trade-off in attack coverage that en-ables certification from finite generative data without global verification of the closed-loop inequalities, while retaining a formal guarantee at the prescribed confidence level. 

Extensibility and future directions. The common pipeline across different dynamics, horizons, action di-mensions, and policy families supports FoSeRL’s portability. Its modular design offers complementary improve-ments: better witness synthesis can reduce representational conservatism, while additional successor samples trade computation for smaller approximation margins. New specifications can be added incrementally using fresh calibration and the confidence-control protocols of Appendix B.7, without predeclaring the entire future experimental family. Alongside richer synthesis and more efficient verification, a natural extension is certificate-aware policy improvement, using witness diagnostics to guide refinement while independently re-certifying each updated policy.

B.7 Scope across tested specifications and collection-level confidence 

Theorem 2 gives a separate confidence statement for each frozen tested specification, including its policy, cou-pling, attack budget, tolerance, operational law, candidate, and sampling design. New specifications may there-fore be added incrementally using fresh calibration data; the complete future experimental family need not be fixed in advance. Simultaneous validity over conformal ranks within one specification does not imply simultaneous validity across different specifications. Consequently, a displayed collection of individually certified points carries no automatic family-wise guarantee, interpolation guarantee, or downward-closure claim when the operational law changes. Likewise, selecting the largest accepted budget after repeated formal tests does not preserve the same nominal confidence for that selected output. For a single selected-budget claim at a stated confidence, candidate selection may use design or previous search data, but the selected specification must then be frozen and assessed using fresh final calibration data. Repeating that final test until acceptance requires additional confidence control. For a simultaneous claim over a finite predeclared collection of 𝐽 specifications, fix all candidates and designs before calibration and choose 𝛽 𝑗 > 0 with Í𝐽 𝑗 =1 𝛽 𝑗 ≤ 𝛽 total . Applying Theorem 2 to specification 𝑗 with 𝛽 =

𝛽 𝑗 and taking a union bound yields probability at least 1 − 𝛽 total that every accepted specification satisfies its own operational target. Independence across specifications is not required, provided each test satisfies its own calibration conditions. The entire future family also need not be predeclared. For sequential testing, one may fix a summable allocation such as 𝛽 𝑗 = 𝛽 total /[ 𝑗 ( 𝑗 + 1)] . Before attempt 𝑗 , freeze its specification using only previously available infor-mation and draw fresh calibration and successor samples conditionally independently of that information. The conditional false-acceptance probability at attempt 𝑗 is then at most 𝛽 𝑗 ; summing these bounds gives the same 1 − 𝛽 total collection-level guarantee over all attempts, including under stopping. Failed attempts still consume their assigned confidence budget. These stronger protocols do not retrospectively apply to runs performed at other confidence levels and do not certify untested or interpolated budgets.

## D Additional Experimental Details and Results 

This appendix gives the complete experimental specification underlying Sec. 5. We first state the benchmark instantiation used in our experiments, then summarize the policy families and the six case studies, describe the certificate parameterization, and finally collect all fixed experimental settings in a single table. Numerical robustness results are reported separately so that benchmark definitions are not mixed with the outcomes of a particular certification run. The complete codebase, experiment configurations, and per-specification results, including the learned certificate parameters, are available at this link. 

23 D.1 Benchmark formulation 

Each case study instantiates the finite-horizon stochastic closed-loop process of Sec. 2. For every tested environment–policy pair, nominal and attacked trajectories start from the same initial state and use the shared episode-randomness semantics of Definition 2. The operational attack law is instantiated separately for each benchmark using the attack method, attacked action channel, and norm budget reported in Table 2. Each real-ized attack is fixed before the episode, perturbs exactly 𝐾 decision times, and satisfies the corresponding per-step norm bound. Thus, 𝐾 controls temporal sparsity, whereas 𝑏 controls the perturbation magnitude. Because the horizons differ across tasks, we report temporal attack density as 𝐾 /𝑇 in addition to the absolute cardinality 𝐾 . The final grids are chosen so that comparable attack densities are represented across environments. The formal event always uses the raw paired-degradation threshold 𝐷 traj ≤ 𝛼 . For cross-task presentation we additionally report 

𝛼 % := 100 𝛼 𝑆 env 

, 𝛼 = 𝛼 %

100 𝑆 env , (97) where 𝑆 env > 0 is a fixed environment-level return scale. The same 𝑆 env is used for every policy compared within one environment. Hence 𝛼 % is neither a success probability nor a policy-specific percentage drop from that policy’s nominal return; it is only a common reporting scale for the raw terminal-degradation threshold. The values used in the current experiments are reported in Table 2. The operating law also fixes the source of episode randomness. In the custom control tasks, the random input enters the declared stochastic state update. For the current Hopper, HalfCheetah, and Ant campaigns, the envi-ronmental randomness follows the corresponding native reset semantics, with no artificial per-step actuation or process noise. Formal verification approximates the conditional successor expectations using fresh Monte Carlo successor batches, with the corresponding sample counts reported in Table 2. 

D.2 Policy families 

The formulation of Sec. 2 is agnostic to how the fixed deployed policy is obtained: 𝜋 may be a learned policy or an analytically designed decision rule, provided its deployment-time execution satisfies Assumption 2. The main empirical study focuses on learned RL policies. Specifically, we evaluate frozen policies trained with TD3 (Fujimoto et al., 2018), SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018), and PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). The exact policy coverage, actor architectures, and deployment conventions are collected in Table 2, while the resulting complete robustness profiles are shown in Fig. 6. Each checkpoint is trained before certification and then held fixed throughout attack generation, paired rollout generation, bar-rier synthesis, calibration, and formal verification. Thus the training algorithm does not alter the certification problem: the certified object is the deployed policy itself, including its chosen deployment-time randomization mode. For the three custom tasks, we additionally retain analytic reference policies: swing-up with local LQR stabi-lization for the inverted pendulum, headway regulation for ACC, and waypoint/pure-pursuit feedback for the unicycle. These are supplementary instances of the same fixed-policy formulation, not competing certification methods. All main-text empirical results report RL policies only.

## E Additional Discussion 

This section expands on Sec. 6, explaining the long-horizon verification design, the information supplied by the barrier witness, and the interpretation and refinement of the resulting certificates. Technical boundary cases and 

> 33

sampling requirements are collected in Appendix B; literature positioning and external-baseline selection are discussed separately in Appendix F. 

E.1 From training-time robustness to long-horizon certification 

The framework of Taheri and Zamani (2026) models stochastic training as a dynamical system and certifies the accuracy of its terminal parameters under data perturbations, already incorporating a training horizon and a time-dependent barrier. FoSeRL changes both the intervention and the certified object: the policy is already fixed, perturbations affect its executed actions, and performance is measured by the accumulated loss relative to a matched nominal episode. The paired-state construction and return-gap coordinate make this sequential objective a terminal safety property, while the separate cardinality and magnitude budgets capture the temporal allocation of attack resources. Long horizons also require controlling the accumulation of local drift allowances. With the earlier linear term 𝑏 3𝑇 , a verification correction 𝜂 3 contributes 𝜂 3𝑇 /𝑏 2 to the failure bound; for example, 

𝜂 3/𝑏 2 = 10 −3 already contributes one at 𝑇 = 1000, exceeding the failure allowance 1 − 𝜌 = 0.1. FoSeRL retains the geometric accumulation G𝑇 (𝜅 ) and calibrates the residual allowance using the final reliability bound. For 

𝜅 < 1, G𝑇 (𝜅 ) ≤ min {𝑇 , (1 − 𝜅 )−1 }, avoiding unnecessary linear accumulation without removing the horizon dependence of the problem (Appendix B.6). Nor can 𝑇 simply be replaced by 𝐾 : an altered state can affect later decisions and rewards after the last attacked action. 

E.2 Episode-level calibration and statistical validity 

FoSeRL calibrates complete episodes rather than treating their dependent transitions as independent observa-tions. The score in Definition 9 aggregates initial and drift residuals while retaining terminal separation as a hard condition. All calibration episodes remain in the sample, including those assigned infinite scores because of unsafe-terminal separation violations. No parametric score distribution is required; validity instead relies on independent episodes and fresh conditional successor samples. Calibration balances the propagated residual margin against the probability of episode-level condition violations. Simultaneous validity over ranks permits selecting this balance from the calibration data without an additional rank-selection penalty. The finite-successor bound then connects the sampled drift residuals to their exact counterparts: conditional on a violating episode, its first exact drift violation is detected with probability at least 1 − 𝛽 0. This yields the transfer penalty ¯ 𝑣 𝑘 ∗ /( 1 − 𝛽 0)

without a union bound over time. Combined with the good-prefix barrier argument, these steps establish the end-to-end operational guarantee of Theorem 2, rather than only marginal coverage of a score. 

E.3 Reliability, confidence, and violation levels 

The target 𝜌 concerns a fresh attack–episode draw: it is the required probability of satisfying 𝐷 traj ≤ 𝛼 under Q𝐾,𝑏 .The reported 𝜌 cert is the lower bound in equation 108, and acceptance requires 𝜌 cert ≥ 𝜌 . In contrast, 𝛽 controls uncertainty over calibration data: conditional on the frozen specification, Theorem 2 bounds false acceptance by 

𝛽 . The auxiliary 𝛽 0 controls the one-sided successor approximation at a fixed state–attack–time tuple through Lemma 1. Its effect is incorporated into the transfer penalty, not deducted again from the outer confidence, which remains 1 − 𝛽 . Finally, ¯ 𝑣 𝑘 ∗ bounds out-of-sample violations of the selected empirical episode conditions; it is nei-ther an empirical failure frequency nor the terminal-degradation risk itself. The latter also includes the dynamical contribution, as made explicit by the Base–Margin–Bridge decomposition in equation 107–equation 108. 

E.4 Barrier witnesses versus direct terminal certification 

FoSeRL provides a dynamical witness in addition to a scalar robustness guarantee. Direct terminal certi-fication applies a one-sided binomial bound to independent observations of the same paired terminal event under the same operational specification. It is a useful formal reference when the required output is only the terminal probability, and can yield larger certified budgets with less computation because it does not require barrier syn-thesis or conditional successor queries. FoSeRL’s additional contribution is the structured certificate supporting that probability and the analysis it enables. 

State–time structure. The witness 𝐵 𝜙 ∗ ( ˆ𝑥, 𝑡 ) links initial conditions, expected one-step evolution, and terminal separation through the conditions of Definition 6. These relations make the dynamics part of the certification argument, rather than reducing each episode solely to a terminal success indicator. The same structure underlies 

> 34

the ideal uniform guarantee and its finite-sample operational counterpart, with their respective scopes stated in Theorems 1 and 2. 

Condition-specific diagnosis and refinement. The residuals in Definition 8 distinguish initial-value, terminal-separation, and drift violations, including the sampled phases at which drift conditions are difficult to satisfy. The reliability decomposition further separates the frozen witness contribution from calibration slack and episode-level violations. This provides concrete directions for refinement: concentrated late-horizon drift vi-olations can motivate targeted synthesis data or time-dependent features, whereas poor terminal separation calls for improving the terminal witness. The scalar binomial certificate alone does not provide this condition-based decomposition. These diagnostics concern the witness, not certified conditional failure probabilities at arbitrary states. In Table 1, FoSeRL retains approximately 71–99% of Direct’s certified magnitude across the representative settings while additionally returning this state–time witness. Thus, the comparison is not between formal and empirical validity: both methods provide formal operational guarantees. It is a trade-off between scalar budget tightness and computation on one side, and a dynamically structured certificate with targeted diagnostic value on the other. When the purpose includes understanding and refining the certifying conditions, the barrier provides information beyond the terminal bound itself. 

E.5 Generality, simulator access, and attack coverage 

FoSeRL separates policy evaluation from policy construction. Under the stated execution and sampling assump-tions, its verification argument does not require a particular learning algorithm, policy architecture, differentiable controller, or analytical dynamics family. The deployed policy remains unchanged, including its chosen random-ization mode; the learned barrier is an auxiliary witness, not a replacement controller. This generality uses a concrete interface: synchronously coupled rollouts and repeatable conditional successor queries, with fresh complete one-step randomness regenerated as specified in Sec. 4. The resulting guarantee concerns the execu-tion process being sampled. The distinction from offline RL and evaluation from single-branch logs is discussed in Appendix F. The operational attack law need not be uniform. It may emphasize difficult precommitted plans or design-selected mixtures, provided it is fixed before calibration and attacks remain independent of the initial state and episode randomness as required by Definition 7. Theorem 2 certifies reliability under this law; uniform coverage of all admissible precommitted attacks instead follows from the ideal barrier conditions. State-adaptive adversaries require an extension of the present attack model. 

E.6 Reading robustness frontiers and selecting specifications 

The robustness profiles expose how attack frequency and magnitude interact with the allowed return loss. Their interpretation retains the declared policy execution, attack channel and norm, horizon, coupling, and statistical target. The PS comparison in Appendix D.8 also retains the distinction between operational and worst-case attack coverage. Normalization by 𝑆 env supplies a common reporting scale within an environment, not a percentage of each policy’s nominal return or a universal ranking across tasks. The empirical design budget supports candi-date construction, whereas an accepted operational budget is supported by independent verification. Neither is asserted to be the optimal robustness radius. The ideal frontier has the nesting properties of Appendix B.3; opera-tional certificates do not automatically inherit them when changing the budget changes the attack law. Connect-ing lines therefore do not certify interpolation or untested specifications. New specifications can nevertheless be added incrementally without predeclaring the entire future experiment family. Each frozen specification receives its own audit; selecting a budget after exploration requires fresh final calibration or appropriate selection-aware confidence control. Appendix B.7 gives the finite-collection and sequential protocols, including accounting for repeated tests and stopping. 

E.7 Performance robustness versus pathwise safety 

Paired degradation complements nominal performance by measuring the loss relative to the same episode with-out an attack. Shared randomness does not force equal actions or states after a perturbation: each branch follows its own closed-loop evolution, preserving the attack’s subsequent consequences. The running gap can increase or recover because individual reward differences can have either sign. Accordingly, crossing the tolerance before 

𝑇 is not itself terminal failure, and the maximum in the verification score aggregates residuals rather than return 

> 35

loss. This formulation certifies cumulative performance without imposing an additional pathwise constraint. Collision avoidance or all-time state safety can be studied alongside it, but requires an explicit safety event and corresponding verification conditions. A safety-violation indicator in the augmented state offers one route for such an extension. 

E.8 Certificate refinement and policy improvement 

Separating construction from verification allows the witness to improve without changing the meaning of ac-ceptance. Richer features, alternative bounded architectures, and targeted synthesis data may improve candidate quality; the synthesis law may emphasize difficult cases rather than match the operational law. Each candi-date and verification design must then be frozen before fresh independent calibration. The decomposition in Appendix D.6 also supports targeted resource allocation. The episode count 𝑁 controls calibration resolution, whereas the successor count 𝑀 controls the approximation correction. The choice of 𝜅 balances geometric prop-agation against the strictness of the local drift inequality. These are distinct design levers, not interchangeable ways to improve a certificate (Appendix B.6). Beyond witness refinement, residual diagnostics offer a direction for certificate-informed policy improvement, with each updated policy treated as a new target and independently re-certified. 

E.9 Limitations and outlook 

FoSeRL’s simulation-based interface supports broad applicability but requires repeated coupled rollouts and con-ditional successor queries. Long horizons and expensive policy or simulator evaluations can therefore increase computational cost, as quantified in Appendix B.6. The reported case studies use the original execution pro-cess without surrogate-assisted synthesis. For larger-scale applications, lightweight surrogates could reduce candidate-construction cost by generating synthesis rollouts. The candidate would still be frozen and formally verified using fresh paired episodes and conditional successors from the original execution process. Provided the verification assumptions and confidence protocol are retained, surrogate accuracy would affect candidate quality and certifiability, not the stated validity of an accepted certificate. Such acceleration would concern synthe-sis; formal verification would retain its original-process sampling requirements. The sufficient barrier conditions also trade some budget tightness for a state–time witness; their conservatism is discussed in Appendix B.4. These computational and tightness considerations accompany the additional certificate structure, while policy and en-vironment generality hold within the execution, sampling, and attack-coverage scope specified in Appendix E.5. The separation between synthesis and verification provides a route to improving efficiency and witness quality while retaining independent formal validation.

For FoSeRL, this denotes a state–time witness and diagnostics, not certified conditional reliability at each state. sampling degenerates once the attacked execution leaves the support of the nominal behaviour. Recovering the paired quantity from logs alone would therefore require the realized randomness together with a simulator, or a learned model able to generate the counterfactual branch. In this respect FoSeRL is closer to high-confidence policy evaluation (Thomas et al., 2015; 2019), though it returns a state- and time-dependent certificate for a degradation tail event rather than a scalar bound on policy value. One connection does carry over. Because the barrier is synthesized and calibrated from sampled coupled trajectories, pointwise readings of it outside regions the operational data represent are extrapolation, echoing the support concerns of offline RL (Fujimoto et al., 2019). This does not affect the distributional certificate, which concerns fresh draws from the declared law, but it bounds how the barrier should be read away from that coverage. Pessimistic regularization (Kumar et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020), learned-model routes to certification from deployment logs (Yu et al., 2020; Kidambi et al., 2020), and certificate-aware policy improvement in the spirit of safe policy improvement (Laroche et al., 2019) are natural directions, and lie outside the present scope.

F.3 Evaluation regime: where smoothing has been tested, and where it has not 

The smoothing line of work has been evaluated primarily on low-dimensional classic-control and Atari bench-marks. Kumar et al. (2022) report Cartpole, Mountain Car, Pong, and Freeway, using DQN for the discrete tasks and DDPG for Mountain Car; Mu et al. (2024) follow a similar evaluation regime on Cartpole, Mountain Car, and Freeway. The only continuous-action environment in these evaluations is Mountain Car, with a single ac-tuated dimension. Neither work reports results on MuJoCo locomotion. This distinction becomes important as the action dimension and attack cardinality increase. Under the aligned smoothing construction used here, the trajectory-level perturbation radius scales as 𝑏 √𝑛 𝑎 𝐾 , so larger 𝑛 𝑎 and 𝐾 make the resulting certified per-step budget increasingly restrictive. Our Hopper and HalfCheetah experiments therefore evaluate the baseline in a substantially higher-dimensional and longer-horizon regime than the settings considered in the original smoothing evaluations. Representative quantitative and qualitative comparisons in these regimes are reported in Appendix D.8.

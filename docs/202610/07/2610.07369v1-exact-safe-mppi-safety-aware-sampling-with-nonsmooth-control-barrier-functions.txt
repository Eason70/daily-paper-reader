# Exact-Safe MPPI: Safety-Aware Sampling with Nonsmooth Control Barrier Functions 

Alexander Coker and Leilei Cui 

Abstract — Model predictive path integral (MPPI) control plans actions by sampling and evaluating trajectories, but standard MPPI does not guarantee safety. Incorporating control barrier function (CBF) filters into each rollout allows the planner to account for safety corrections. However, combining multiple CBFs through a soft-minimum approximation can exclude states that satisfy the original safety constraints, re-stricting the trajectories available to the planner. This paper proposes Exact-Safe MPPI, which uses the exact minimum of multiple barrier functions and applies a CBF-based quadratic program during both planning and control execution. To handle nondifferentiability where multiple barriers attain the minimum, the filter simultaneously enforces the CBF conditions for all δ-active barriers, whose values lie within a prescribed tolerance δ > 0 of the minimum. Under suitable feasibility and regularity assumptions, we establish forward invariance of the certified safe set without the shrinkage introduced by soft-minimum smoothing. We also characterize the state-dependent approximation gap and explain how multiple barriers with values at or near the minimum increase this shrinkage. Further-more, we show that increasing the number of MPPI samples cannot recover trajectories excluded by the soft-minimum filter. We demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach in a navigation task requiring passage through a narrow gap between two obstacles. Video demonstrations are available at 

https://github.com/lc-lab25/Exact-Safe-MPPI .

Index Terms — Control barrier functions, sampling-based control, nonsmooth analysis. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Model predictive control (MPC) is an optimization-based feedback control approach that repeatedly plans over a finite horizon. At each time step, it uses the current system state to compute a control sequence that minimizes a specified cost while satisfying the system dynamics and constraints, including safety requirements. Only the first control input is applied, and the process repeats using the updated state. Initially adopted in industrial process control, MPC has since found widespread use in robotics, transportation, and other fields [1], [2]. However, solving the underlying optimization problem can be computationally demanding, particularly for nonlinear systems with nonsmooth or discontinuous costs and complex constraints. Building on path integral methods for stochastic optimal control [3], model predictive path integral (MPPI) control offers a sampling-based approach within the same receding-horizon framework [4], [5]. At each planning step, MPPI samples candidate control sequences, simulates the resulting trajectories, and updates the control sequence by assigning greater weight to samples with lower costs. These simulations can run in parallel without requiring

derivatives of the dynamics or cost, and can accommodate nonsmooth or discontinuous costs, making MPPI attractive for robotic applications [6], [7]. However, standard MPPI may generate many trajectories that violate the robot’s safety constraints, spending substantial computation on unsafe can-didate trajectories that contribute little to the control update. To improve the safety of MPPI, one can penalize constraint violations in the objective function [4] or bias sampling toward trajectories that remain within the safe region [8]. These approaches encourage safe behavior but do not, by themselves, guarantee safety. They may also spend substan-tial computation evaluating unsafe trajectories that contribute little to the control update. Another approach combines MPPI with control barrier function (CBF)-based safety mecha-nisms [9]–[11]. If a safety filter modifies the proposed control but these modifications are not considered during planning, the predicted trajectories may differ from the resulting motion. This mismatch can degrade performance and lead to repeated interventions, oscillations, or “chattering” [12]– [15]. Accounting for the safety filter during planning can help reduce these effects. In [10], CBF conditions guide the selection of the mean and covariance of the sampled controls through a semidefinite program, which can be more computationally demanding than a standard QP-based CBF filter. GS-MPPI [11] instead applies a QP-based CBF filter to the sampled controls within each rollout. MPPI therefore evaluates trajectories generated by the filtered controls and accounts for the filter when updating its proposed control sequence. To handle multiple safety constraints, GS-MPPI combines their barrier functions into a single smooth barrier using a soft-minimum approximation. However, combining multiple CBFs through a soft-minimum approximation can exclude trajectories in the exact certified set, causing the planner to treat an otherwise feasible solution as infeasible, as illustrated in Section IV.B. In this paper, we propose Exact-Safe MPPI, a framework that incorporates multiple CBFs into MPPI using their exact minimum rather than a soft-minimum approximation (see Fig. 2). During planning, a CBF-QP filter modifies the sampled controls within each rollout. The same filter is applied to the control proposed by MPPI before it is executed on the system. III. E XACT CONSTRAINTS COMPOSITION 

Since hρ is a soft-minimum approximation of hmin sat-isfying hρ(x) ≤ hmin (x), the soft-minimum construction certifies the smaller set H ⊆ D . This inclusion can be strict, introducing conservatism in the certified safe set. To address this conservatism, we work directly with the exact minimum 

hmin , which may be nondifferentiable at points where multi-ple component barriers attain the minimum. At such points, its Clarke generalized gradient is the convex hull of the gradients of the active component barriers [24]. Enforcing a condition for only one active barrier does not, in general, ensure that the other active constraints are satisfied [17]. We therefore develop a construction that simultaneously enforces all δ-active constraints. We refer to the resulting method as 

Exact-Safe MPPI .

B. Forward invariance under simultaneous enforcement 

For each component barrier, enforcing the highest-order CBF condition in (6) preserves the lower-order intermedi-ate barrier inequalities, provided they hold initially. Thus, enforcing these conditions for all component barriers ren-ders D, defined in (7), forward invariant [21], [22]. This result applies separately to each component barrier, includ-ing when the components have different relative degrees. It therefore remains only to show that the QP enforces the required highest-order condition whenever a component barrier reaches its boundary. Because the δ-active set can change discontinuously at tie states, closed-loop solutions are interpreted in the Filippov sense [25]. 

Theorem 1 (Forward invariance of D): Under Assump-tion 1, let δ > 0, and suppose that the QP (16) is feasible in a neighborhood of D and returns a measurable, locally bounded control ˆu with locally bounded slack vector ˆμ. Then every Filippov solution of 

˙x = f (x) + g(x)ˆ u(x)

starting in D remains in D throughout its interval of exis-tence. 

Proof: Consider a highest-order component barrier 

bj,d j −1 at a state ¯x ∈ D for which bj,d j −1(¯ x) = 0 . Since every highest-order barrier is nonnegative in D, hmin (¯ x) = 0 ,and therefore j ∈ Aδ (¯ x). By continuity of bj,d j −1 and δ > 0,the component bj,d j −1 remains δ-active in a neighborhood of ¯x.At the boundary, the slack term in (16) vanishes. Thus, the QP constraint gives the unrelaxed component CBF condition 

Lf bj,d j −1 + Lg bj,d j −1 ˆu + α(bj,d j −1) ≥ 0.

Because ˆμ is locally bounded, the slack contribution 

ˆμj bj,d j −1 vanishes as bj,d j −1 → 0. The resulting boundary inequality is preserved under the Filippov convexification of the closed-loop dynamics. The standard comparison argu-ment [25], [26] therefore prevents bj,d j −1 from becoming negative. The standard high-order CBF recursion then implies that nonnegativity of every final barrier function preserves the lower-order intermediate barrier functions defining D [21], [22]. Hence every condition defining D remains satisfied, and the trajectory stays in D.

A. Gap-closing experiment 

The theory predicts that soft-minimum smoothing can close a gap that remains available under the exact barrier composition. We test whether the predicted threshold κcrit =ln 2 identifies when this occurs in practice. We use a shared double-integrator vehicle model with two obstacle geometries: rounded-square obstacles represented by p-norm barriers ( p = 8 ), and circular obstacles repre-sented by squared-distance barriers. The vehicle must pass between the obstacles to reach its goal. We vary the gap width and the soft-minimum parameter ρ, then compare soft-minimum GS-MPPI [11] with Exact-Safe MPPI. Each condition uses 50 random seeds. In total, the experiment contains 4400 soft-minimum runs and 550 exact-composition runs. We call a run threaded when it crosses the required slice X

between the obstacles. A run completes when it also reaches the goal. We exclude the narrowest disk gap because the exact-composition planner crossed the gap in 60% of runs but never reached the goal; that case tests goal reachability rather than soft-minimum gap closure. B. Nine-constraint comparison 

The gap-closing experiment involves only two obstacle barriers. We next test whether simultaneously enforcing all 

δ-active barriers matters in a more complicated map with nine constraints. The GS-MPPI paper [11] does not specify the obstacle geometry used in its benchmark. We therefore reconstruct an approximate version from the published figure using rounded-square obstacles. This is a controlled reconstruction, not an exact replication of the original benchmark. The published temperature λ = 1 makes the first MPPI update in our reconstruction depend almost entirely on one rollout. We therefore use λ = 300 for every controller in this comparison so that the update uses many rollouts. All controller settings use the same costs, random seeds, integration method, and step sizes. We compare soft-minimum GS-MPPI over several values of ρ with Exact-Safe MPPI. We also test a single-constraint version of the exact filter that enforces only the most re-strictive barrier at each state, rather than all δ-active barriers together. Each controller setting is tested from four goals using 100 random seeds per goal. Exact-Safe MPPI produced no recorded safety violations in the nine-constraint map. Soft-minimum GS-MPPI pro-duced no violations at ρ ∈ {5, 20 , 50 }, followed by 34 

violations at ρ = 200 and 53 violations at ρ = 1000 .These large-ρ failures are numerical: although hρ remains differentiable for finite ρ, it becomes difficult to evaluate accurately near barrier ties as ρ becomes very large. The single-constraint filter produced safety violations and greater control chattering. In contrast, simultaneously enforc-ing all δ-active component barriers produced no violations in the tested runs. This supports the need for simultaneous enforcement in the proposed filter. VI. D ISCUSSION 

The safety result in Theorem 1 applies to the continuous-time filtered system and does not directly extend to its Euler discretization. With a sufficiently large integration step, a numerical trajectory may leave the certified safe set even when the corresponding continuous-time trajectory remains safe [29]. In our experiments, refining the execution integration step reduced these errors, and the executed Exact-Safe MPPI trajectories at δt = 2 × 10 −3 s did not leave D.The guarantee remains conditional on the QP being feasible and returning locally bounded control and slack values. The support argument shows that soft-minimum filtering can exclude trajectories that pass through D \ H . When the lowest-cost exact-safe trajectory requires such a region, the soft-minimum planner can have an unavoidable cost gap. This result does not show that Exact-Safe MPPI finds the globally lowest-cost trajectory in D, nor that it recovers every trajectory reachable under the exact filter. The reconstructed nine-constraint benchmark contains no goal in D \ H . The rounded-square and disk experiments therefore isolate the gap-closing mechanism, but they do not establish how often comparable certified-set shrinkage affects other environments, cost functions, or sampling distributions. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented Exact-Safe MPPI, which incorpo-rates an exact-minimum CBF-QP filter into both trajectory sampling and control execution of MPPI. By simultane-ously enforcing the conditions for all δ-active barriers, the framework accommodates multiple high-order CBFs and preserves the certified safe set under the stated feasibility and regularity assumptions. We characterized the state-dependent gap introduced by soft-minimum smoothing and showed how it can exclude safe passages from every rollout. Increas-ing the number of samples cannot recover these excluded paths. Navigation experiments supported this analysis: no soft-minimum rollout threaded the gap below the predicted threshold, while exact composition allowed passage with-out recorded safety violations in the tested cases. A nine-constraint comparison further demonstrated the importance of enforcing multiple active barriers simultaneously. Future work will address safety guarantees for sampled-data imple-mentations.

Abstract 

Environmental robotic sampling requires considering the dual influence of water currents on robotic motion and particle transport. Existing marine robotics simulators generally model flow, autonomy, and sampling targets separately, limiting joint evaluation of mission cost and sampling performance. H-SPAR integrates spatially and temporally varying velocity fields, Lagrangian particle transport, probabilistic sampling, and ROS 2/Gazebo-based uncrewed surface vehicle (USV) autonomy. In this work, shared precomputed flow fields drive particle advection and current-induced forces during closed-loop vehicle execu-tion. Path-planning experiments show that the existing current-aware planner SVF-RRT* achieves 69.4% lower upstream cost than conventional RRT* at the planning level, but this reduction falls to 41.7% during execution under time-varying currents, reflecting temporal flow variation, vehicle motion constraints, and path deviation omitted during planning. Coverage experiments show that sweep orientation changes the particle-sampling rate by up to 22.2% under the complete H-SPAR configuration. These findings highlight the importance of eval-uating planning, vehicle execution, particle transport, and sampling together under consistent hydrodynamic conditions. The project webpage is available at https://sites.google.com/view/h-spar, and the open-source code is available on GitHub at https://github.com/naviiidz/h-spar-sim.

1arXiv:2610.01985v1 [cs.RO] 1 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Marine field robotics has become increasingly important for environmental monitor-ing, disaster response, and localized pollutant sampling across aquatic ecosystems. A key challenge in these applications is executing autonomous sampling missions, such as microplastic tracking, oil spill monitoring, or harmful algal bloom characteriza-tion, where spatially and temporally varying flow fields actively dictate both system dynamics and target availability. The varying flow in water currents alter uncrewed surface vehicle (USV) trajectories, mission duration, and propulsion energy, while simultaneously advecting and redistributing the floating particles or pollutants to be sampled. Therefore, modeling this dual influence of hydrodynamic flow fields is essen-tial for designing effective autonomous sampling strategies and evaluating true mission performance in real-world marine environments. Spatially and temporally varying currents create non-convex search spaces where geometrically optimal paths frequently lead to severe trajectory drift, high propul-sion expenditure, or actuation saturation. At the same time, because target particles continuously advect with the underlying flow field, the spatial distribution of targets evolves during mission execution. Consequently, evaluating a robot’s sampling per-formance requires accounting for relative fluid-intake velocities and transient particle distributions. Simulating these coupled interactions accurately without incurring the high computational cost of full online Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) presents a major bottleneck for current-aware autonomy research. Prior research has addressed flow-aware marine robotics from complementary but largely separate perspectives. On the algorithmic side, vector-field path planners such as VF-RRT [1] and SVF-RRT [2] optimize trajectories with respect to prescribed envi-ronmental flow fields, while other works explicitly consider uncertainty in time-varying currents [3, 4]. These algorithms [1–4] rely on point-mass abstractions while ignor-ing rigid-body hydrodynamics, focusing primarily on geometric path optimization or energy cost. Likewise, energy-aware coverage approaches often impose simplified envi-ronmental assumptions. For example, Zhao et al. [5] analyze sweep-direction selection under a spatially uniform current direction inside USV surveying region of interest. Furthermore, frameworks incorporating temporal updates in water currents discretize hourly forecasts into coarse spatial grids, limiting USV mission simulations to static, low-resolution flow-field representations [6]. On the simulation side, established platforms such as DAVE [7] (extending UUV Simulator [8]) include aquatic features such as ocean current models (constant, strati-fied, and tidal), dynamic bathymetry spawning, and physics-based underwater sensors for underwater robots. Similarly, the Virtual RobotX (VRX) simulator [9] extends Gazebo with wave, wind, buoyancy, and surface vessel dynamics models to sup-port autonomy testing on USVs, with potential of using Gazebo plugins for adding water currents on USVs. In addition, MOOS-IvP [10] has been widely used for USV mission planning and multi-vehicle coordination, offering a mature autonomy mid-dleware with integrated simulation capabilities. MOOS-IvP uses additional current toolboxes to accept externally published drift vectors. Photorealistic simulators such as MARUS [11] and UNav-Sim [12] prioritize visual rendering of aquatic environ-ments for perception tasks. As a result, researchers cannot jointly evaluate USV trajectory execution, energy expenditure, and environmental sampling efficiency within a single closed-loop simulator. In marine environments, suspended particles and USVs are influenced by the same underlying flow field. Evaluating particle-sampling missions therefore requires the joint simulation of flow-driven particle transport and current-affected USV motion. Although recent studies have investigated robotic platforms for in situ monitoring of particles and plumes [13, 14], existing marine robotics simulators provide no support for jointly modeling hydrodynamic particle transport, flow-dependent robot execution, and sampling-oriented mission planning. Such joint simulation can be performed at different levels of physical fidelity. Particle-based fluid methods can represent detailed fluid behavior and particle-scale interactions; however, enforcing physical properties such as incompressibility introduces substantial computational demands [15]. Explic-itly resolving these details for large particle populations is therefore not well suited to mission-scale marine simulations, where the primary objective is to capture particle transport and spatial distribution. To address these limitations, we propose H-SPAR, an open-source and hydrodynamic-aware simulation framework for particle transport and autonomous marine robotics. # 2.1 Environmental Velocity Field 

H-SPAR represents the hydrodynamic environment using fine-grained velocity fields that describe the local water motion. These fields can be obtained from different sources, including physics-based hydrodynamic solvers [18], analytical flow models [2], and data-driven predictors [19], depending on the target environment and the desired trade-off between computational cost and fidelity. H-SPAR is therefore independent of the method used to generate the velocity fields, provided that the resulting data contain the spatial and temporal information required by the simulation. In this work, the computational domain is discretized using an unstructured trian-gular mesh generated with Gmsh [20]. The unstructured representation accommodates irregular shorelines, concave regions, and obstacle boundaries [21], while allowing local mesh refinement in regions with complex geometry or strong flow gradients [22]. The resulting velocity fields are exported and subsequently used by H-SPAR for particle advection, current-aware path evaluation, and USV mission simulation. To balance hydrodynamic fidelity and computational scalability, the velocity fields used in this study are generated using the depth-averaged Shallow Water Equations (SWE) [23]. This formulation captures large-scale horizontal flow structures while remaining computationally tractable for mission-level robotic simulation. Because the equations describe depth-averaged horizontal motion, they do not resolve vertical velocity profiles, stratification, three-dimensional turbulence, or near-body fluid inter-actions. These assumptions are appropriate for the surface-level transport scenarios 6considered here, in which particle advection and USV motion are primarily influenced by large-scale horizontal currents. 

# 2.2 Current-Aware Path Planning 

Water currents can substantially affect the hydrodynamic difficulty, feasibility, and duration of marine robotic missions. A path that is geometrically short may require prolonged upstream motion and therefore be difficult to execute efficiently. H-SPAR represents this effect using an upstream-cost metric [2] that depends on the motion of the USV relative to the local current field. H-SPAR implements and evaluates the geometry-based RRT* planner and two current-aware variants, VF-RRT* [1] and SVF-RRT* [2]. RRT* considers path length and obstacle avoidance, whereas VF-RRT* and SVF-RRT* additionally query the environmental velocity field along candidate path segments. This allows the planners to favor trajectories that are more aligned with the local flow and avoid regions with strong opposing currents. All planners use the same static navigation costmap to represent traversable water, land boundaries, obstacles, and no-go regions. Each planner outputs a sequence of waypoints, which is passed to the ROS 2 execution node. This separation enables H-SPAR to compare the cost of the planned path with the behavior of the trajectory after execution under vehicle constraints and current-induced disturbances. To quantify the hydrodynamic difficulty of both planned and executed trajectories, H-SPAR uses a discrete upstream-cost metric adapted from Eq. (1) of SVF-RRT* [2]. At the planning level, the metric is evaluated along the generated path using the local velocity field. 

# 2.3 Current-Aware USV Dynamics 

H-SPAR estimates current-induced drag based on the spatial and temporal state of each USV. At each simulation time step, the framework queries the vehicle position and simulation timestamp. It then retrieves the corresponding local current velocity and computes the resulting drag force and moment. Accurately modeling hydrodynamic drag is essential for simulating USV dynam-ics in current-influenced environments. We adopt a quadratic damping formulation commonly used in marine robotics to represent surge, sway, and yaw effects [9]. This formulation is suitable for control-oriented USV models, where hydrodynamic coefficients and mass properties are often uncertain and difficult to characterize analytically [24]. In our design, the USV features two hulls in a catamaran layout, providing a wide stance and reduced drag. # 2.4 Gazebo-Based Simulation, Execution, and Metrics 

Gazebo provides the physics-based simulation frontend of H-SPAR. The Gazebo ecosystem includes reusable marine environments, USV models, sensors, obstacles, and plugins for buoyancy and hydrodynamic effects. It also simulates rigid-body dynamics, propulsion, collision handling, and onboard sensor measurements. During simulation, the pose and velocity of the USV are continuously transmit-ted through the ROS 2-Gazebo bridge. An H-SPAR ROS 2 node uses the vehicle state and simulation timestamp to query the local current field. Before simulation, a 8KD-tree [26], implemented using SciPy [27], is constructed from the fixed mesh coordi-nates to enable efficient spatial indexing. Since the mesh geometry remains unchanged throughout the simulation, the KD-tree is generated only once during initialization. At runtime, the vehicle position is queried against the KD-tree to obtain the index of the nearest mesh point, and the corresponding velocity vector is retrieved using the simulation timestamp and spatial index. The corresponding drag force and moment are computed using the formulation described in Sec. 2.3. The resulting hydrodynamic wrench is then applied to the USV model through the bridge. For low-level vehicle actuation, H-SPAR adopts the thrust-mapping and model-based control approach of Saldarriaga et al. [17]. This method converts linear and angular velocity references into individual commands for thrusters. H-SPAR uses this existing controller as part of the execution pipeline and does not introduce a new actuator-control formulation. During each mission, H-SPAR records planning-level and execution-level data to evaluate geometric path cost, hydrodynamic difficulty, and trajectory feasibility. At the planning level, the planned path length is computed as the total Euclidean distance between consecutive waypoints generated by the path planner. The planned upstream cost is evaluated along the same waypoint sequence using the current-aware cost formulation described in Sec. 2.2. At the execution level, H-SPAR records the actual trajectory length, mission dura-tion, local current velocity, applied hydrodynamic wrench, and executed upstream cost. The executed upstream cost is evaluated along the recorded trajectory using the same formulation applied at the planning level. This enables direct comparison between the predicted cost of a planned path and its realized performance during simulation. To quantify deviation from the planned path, each executed position is compared with the closest segment of the planned polyline. The point-wise path deviation is defined as

# 2.5 Lagrangian Particle Transport 

The fine-grained Eulerian velocity fields are interpolated at each particle location to update the motion of Lagrangian particles in the water. Particle transport accounts for both advection by the local flow velocity and diffusion [28].

This model pre-vents numerical diffusion, which smooths sharp concentration gradients and obscures fine-scale structures common in Eulerian methods [29]. H-SPAR adopts a one-way coupling assumption: the environmental velocity field affects both Lagrangian particle transport and USV motion, whereas disturbances induced by the vehicle are not fed back into the flow field or particle dynamics. Con-sequently, localized near-body effects, including wake generation, propeller wash, and vehicle-induced particle disturbance, are not resolved. This approximation is suit-able for the mission-scale simulations considered here, where vehicle-induced flow is assumed to remain localized relative to the spatial extent of the environmental current field. Resolving these interactions would require two-way fluid–structure coupling or local CFD around the vehicle and is left for future work. 

# 2.6 Probabilistic Particle Sampling Model 

To evaluate particle-monitoring missions, H-SPAR includes a mission-level proba-bilistic sampling model. The model does not resolve the detailed fluid-particle-pump interaction; instead, it estimates whether particles encountered by the USV are sampled as a function of their relative motion with respect to the robot. # 3 Experiments 

H-SPAR supports the evaluation of energy-constrained USV operations and pollutant-sampling strategies. This section presents two experiments designed to demonstrate these capabilities. First, current-aware mission planners are evaluated at both the planning and execution levels, and their performance is compared using the proposed metrics (Sec. 3.1). Second, H-SPAR is used to investigate how mission design influences particle-sampling performance in coverage-based monitoring tasks (Sec. 3.2). In all experiments, the USV model is constrained by maximum linear and angular velocities of 8 m/s and 1.5 rad/s, and maximum linear and angular accelerations of 2 m/s 2 and 1 rad/s 2, respectively. For local trajectory execution, we adopt the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA), a widely used method in mobile robotics [31]. DWA explicitly considers kinematic constraints while generating smooth and feasible trajectories. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with a 13th-generation Intel Core i7-13700K processor with 16 physical cores and 24 threads, 16 GB of memory, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 GPU. 

# 3.1 Experiment 1: Current-Aware Planning and Execution 

This experiment evaluates the paths generated by the current-aware planners and compares their predicted performance during planning with their realized performance during mission execution. Three planners are compared: the geometry-based RRT* baseline [32], VF-RRT* [1], and SVF-RRT* [2], as illustrated in Fig. 3. All plan-ners generate a waypoint sequence using the same static costmap, while VF-RRT* and SVF-RRT* additionally incorporate the environmental velocity field into their planning costs. Since these planners do not explicitly consider temporal variations in the flow field, the time-averaged velocity field over all simulation timestamps is used during planning. The waypoint sequence generated by each path planner is subsequently passed to the same DWA motion planner for execution. The DWA accounts for the kine-matic constraints of the USV, allowing the performance of the planned paths to be assessed under a common execution framework. At the planning level, path length and upstream cost are evaluated. At the execution level, the resulting trajectory length, mission time, path deviation, and executed upstream cost are reported. As shown in Table 2, RRT* produces the shortest planned path but also incurs the highest planned upstream cost. This result demonstrates that minimizing geometric path length alone does not necessarily yield a favorable trajectory in the presence of environmental currents. # 3.2 Experiment 2: Coverage-Based Particle Monitoring 

This experiment evaluates coverage-based particle monitoring under hydrodynamic conditions, with emphasis on sampling performance rather than solely on USV motion. In current-influenced environments, coverage planning requires several mission-level decisions, including the extent of the coverage region, sweep orientation, line spacing, and vehicle heading configuration. These choices cannot be determined from geome-try alone because the surrounding flow affects mission duration, current-related cost, particle transport, and the likelihood of particle encounters during execution. In this experiment, we have tested the effect of sweep pattern on sampling per-formance. We employ boustrophedon sweep-based strategy to generate waypoints for 13 systematic area monitoring [33]. In H-SPAR, boustrophedon waypoints are automat-ically generated within a user-defined region of interest using the method described by Zhao et al. [5]. Different sweep orientations are then executed under the pre-scribed velocity field and particle distribution to evaluate their effect on monitoring and sampling performance (Fig. 4). To maintain a continuous and comparable particle influx throughout the exper-iment, 100 particles are released from the upstream boundary every 10 s. This enables the influence of hydrody-namic particle transport on the resulting spatial particle distribution and sampling opportunities to be evaluated (Fig. 4b-d).

# 4 Conclusion 

This paper presented H-SPAR, a hydrodynamic-aware simulation framework that inte-grates flow-field modeling, Lagrangian particle transport, and ROS 2/Gazebo-based USV autonomy using a shared environmental representation. Experiments demon-strated that the shortest geometric path is not necessarily the most effective under spatially varying currents, while flow-aware planners can reduce upstream motion and improve mission performance at the cost of potential tracking deviations. Coverage experiments further showed that the preferred sweep orientation depends on mission duration, hydrodynamic cost, particle transport, and the sampling model, particu-larly when capture probability is governed by particle–vehicle relative velocity. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating marine monitoring strategies using planning-, execution-, and sampling-level metrics rather than geometric measures or total particle counts alone. Future work will validate simulated particle transport and vehicle drift against physical data, extend H-SPAR to three-dimensional and dynam-ically updated flow fields, incorporate higher-fidelity sensing and capture models, and evaluate additional rule-based and learning-based planning strategies. 16 Data availability

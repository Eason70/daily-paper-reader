# Learning in Inverse Games: Tractable Training with Probabilistic Guarantees 

Arghya Mallick, Reza Rahimi Baghbadorani, Peyman Mohajerin Esfahani, and Sergio Grammatico 

Abstract. Inverse game theory seeks to learn agents’ unknown objectives from observed equilib-rium behavior. Existing residual-based approaches can lead to non-convex problems and need not ensure strong monotonicity of the learned game, limiting reliable equilibrium prediction. We develop a tractable convex framework for learning static and dynamic non-cooperative games using first-order and Nikaido-Isoda (NI) loss. Specialized operator parameterizations, including a Helmholtz– Hodge decomposition in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), enforce strong monotonicity for quadratic, nonparametric, non-quadratic, and linear-quadratic dynamic games. We establish finite-sample out-of-sample prediction guarantees, using Rademacher complexity to match existing bounds for the non-quadratic setting. For noisy sequential data, we develop a robust receding-horizon learn-ing scheme whose adaptive regularization admits a maximum a posteriori (MAP) interpretation and is computed using randomized trace estimation. Numerical experiments, including a stylized autonomous-vehicle collision-avoidance application, demonstrate predictive accuracy and robustness to measurement noise. 

1. Introduction 

In multi-agent non-cooperative environments, self-interested entities optimize individual utilities that depend on the joint actions of their peers. This strategic interaction is naturally formalized as a non-cooperative game. Across diverse applications, such as network resource allocation [17], peer-to-peer energy trading in power systems [5], and autonomous driving [27], these interactions are modeled as games wherein the emergent operational behavior coincides with a Nash equilibrium. From the perspective of a central system operator, anticipating these equilibrium profiles is critical for ensuring operational efficiency and system reliability. This raises a fundamental system-theoretic question: can a designer reliably infer the underlying structure of a game from observations of its equilibria? More specifically, can the private objective functions of the agents be learned? While inverse optimization theory [21, 30] affirmatively resolves this for single-agent systems, inverse game

It establishes a rigorous mathematical frame-work to learn the unknown objective functions of self-interested agents using historical datasets of exogenous signals and their corresponding Nash equilibrium actions. To illustrate the practical utility of inverse game theory, consider network resource allocation [17] within cloud computing infrastructures. The system operator is the cloud provider, while the competing agents comprise bandwidth-intensive services, such as video transcoders and database synchronizers. These agents seek to maximize their individual data throughput across a shared, capacity-constrained network, thereby inducing a non-cooperative resource-sharing game. By con-tinuously monitoring exogenous network signals (e.g., background traffic loads) alongside the re-sulting equilibrium bandwidth allocations, the provider can utilize inverse game theory to learn the hidden utility functions of these services. Consequently, when confronted with a disruptive future state, such as an anticipated surge in prime-time traffic, the provider can accurately forecast the emergent Nash equilibrium actions. This predictive capability enables the proactive implementation of traffic-shaping measures to mitigate congestion and avert probable network outages. The problem of inferring objective functions from observed behavior traces its roots to single-agent inverse optimal control and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) [18, 23]. In continuous control do-mains, bilevel optimization frameworks are widely adopted. These approaches typically embed the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions of the forward problem as constraints [22] or directly minimize the condition residuals evaluated at the demonstrated trajectories [12]. While extensions to multi-agent IRL exist, they largely assume potential game structures and do not generalize to arbitrary non-cooperative settings. To address this, recent literature has formalized inverse dynamic games [1, 19, 29]. A prevalent methodology extends single-agent residual minimiza-tion by penalizing the violation of first-order necessary conditions for open-loop Nash equilibria [1]. Beyond resulting in highly non-convex optimization problems [19], an important limitation of these methods is the absence of inherent guarantees that the learned parameters will yield a monotone game during forward prediction. Moreover, the methodology in [29] is limited to an unconstrained two-player dynamic game. In a foundational contribution aligned with inverse game theory, the authors of [8] proposed a tractable convex formulation for inverse variational inequalities, offering both parametric and nonparametric approaches to estimate the pseudogradient operator. However, their methodology does not enforce monotonicity on the learned pseudogradient operator, a crit-ical property required for the convergence of standard Nash equilibrium-seeking algorithms [28]. Overcoming limitations of existing approaches [1, 8, 19, 29], we propose a tractable inverse game formulation equipped with formal statistical guarantees for predicting the Nash equilibria of the learned game. Specifically, our main contributions are as follows. (i) Tractable convex formulations: We develop tractable convex formulations for three classes of inverse games: (a) Exact reformulation with NI loss. For inverse linear-quadratic games, we introduce the Nikaido– Isoda (NI) loss and derive an exact convex reformulation with LMI constraints (Proposi-tion 3.4). 3

(b) Non-quadratic monotone games. For inverse non-quadratic monotone games, we parameterize the game operator in an RKHS using the Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition and reduce the resulting infinite-dimensional inverse problem to a finite-dimensional convex program with second-order cone constraints (Theorem 4.3). (c) Noisy data in dynamic games. For inverse linear-quadratic dynamic games, we enforce strong monotonicity via LMIs and mitigate noise propagation in sequential data through a Tikhonov-regularized receding-horizon formulation. With an appropriate dynamic update of the reg-ularization penalty, its solution coincides with the Bayesian MAP estimate of the noise-free parameter (Proposition 5.7). (ii) Probabilistic guarantees: For both inverse quadratic and non-quadratic monotone games, we establish finite-sample guarantees on the out-of-sample predictive accuracy of the learned forward game (Theorems 3.5 and 4.4). For the latter, exploiting strong monotonicity and Rademacher complexity yields a statistical bound matching the best-known guarantee for the inverse varia-tional inequality framework [8, Theorem 8].

4. Inverse Formulation for Non-Quadratic Monotone Games 

Expanding the hypothesis space to capture more general functions is a practical imperative for learning complex forward games. However, one must balance expressivity with the necessity of a tractable inverse formulation. Furthermore, to ensure compatibility with popular Nash equilibrium-seeking algorithms [28], we require the pseudogradient operator of the learned game to remain (strongly) monotone. To achieve this, we synthesize monotonicity constraints with nonparametric structures drawn from infinite-dimensional reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). 4.1. Proposed formulation

Definition 4.1 (Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition) . Let F be a sufficiently smooth vector field on a bounded domain Ξ with a smooth boundary ∂Ξ. Then, F can be uniquely decomposed as

# MOSAIC-SV: Real-Time Adaptive Identification of Vessel Dynamics for the Control and Deployment of Aquatic Robots 

Wensen Liu 1, Jerry Peng 1, Shravani Vedagiri 1, and Aaron M. Johnson 1

Abstract — Model-based control of an aquatic robotic plat-form depends on a hydrodynamic model that is costly to identify and specific to the hull, payload, and conditions it was measured in. Here, we present MOSAIC-SV, a deployable real-time adaptive dynamics identification and control system that identifies a control-sufficient dynamics model from aspec-sheet engineering prior, without dedicated identification trials, and re-estimates it at every control step of a closed-loop mission while the controller plans on it. A physically admissible unscented Kalman filter re-estimates hydrodynamic, disturbance, and actuator parameters at every control step of the closed-loop mission; while a command-dependent consider projection withholds corrections the current command cannot attribute between actuator effectiveness and external force; and a model predictive path integral controller plans on the current estimate. In simulation on a CyberShip II plant, MOSAIC-SV recovers the transit performance of the calibrated model under static mismatch and transient changes, and stays within 

20% of its own transit time at the unscaled prior when its inertia or damping prior is wrong by an order of magnitude. In on-water field trials on the Blue Robotics BlueBoat, a twin-thruster catamaran, MOSAIC-SV transits at least 25% faster and predicts its own motion with at least 56% less error than its frozen engineering prior, including under an unmodeled payload. The same MOSAIC-SV system concept was also feasibly deployed on a 6.3-tonne dual outboard monohull. 

I. INTRODUCTION Aquatic robotic platforms such as unmanned surface vehi-cles (USVs) have great potential to aid in marine conserva-tion [1], search and rescue [2], and exploration [3]. Model-based control of a USV depends on a model of the hydrody-namic effects in its dynamics [4]. Current USV controllers typically use parametric physics-based maneuvering models (e.g. Fossen [5], MMG [6]) paired with predictive controllers [7], [8] or learned policies [9]. However, these models are non-trivial to derive and calibrate [10], particularly for unconventional hullforms and marine robots [11]. Several approaches address this. One approach is to identify the model a priori from data, through parametric identification [12] or a learned dynamics model [13], and give the controller the identified model. Abrougui et al. [14] identify a USV model from sea trials and design heading and speed controllers on it. Wang et al. [15] identify the maneuvering model with an extended Kalman filter on simulated excitation data before control, then run a disturbance observer alongside a nonlinear MPC

In each, the data collected and subsequent model are specific to that vessel and the conditions it was operated in; a change to the hull, payload, actuator, or sea state requires a new identification. A second approach is to learn a correction on top of a known nominal model during operation. Zhang et al. [16] learn the mismatch between a nominal model and the measured yaw response online and add it to the prediction model of an MPC. Liu et al. [17] learn per-axis residual dynamics with a sparse Gaussian process that is refined recursively inside a nonlinear MPC. Outside the marine domain, Levy et al. [18] couple Kalman-filter adaptation of a dynamics model to MPPI on an off-road vehicle, but adapt along a basis meta-learned from previously collected vehicle-specific driving data. These methods, however, require a known nominal model of the test platform and require ded-icated identification trials to train or calibrate the correction algorithms before deployment. A third approach is to identify the physical model itself online, which previous work has explored as an estimation problem. Hydrodynamic coefficients are estimated as aug-mented states with an extended [19] or unscented Kalman filter [20], [21], with some estimators also identifying pro-peller and rudder force coefficients [21]. These studies pri-marily use recorded maneuvering-test data or simulation data and, when applicable, give a controller a fixed model [22], [21]. A. Contribution 

In this work we present MOSAIC-SV ( Maritime Onboard 

System for Adaptive Identification and Control of Surface 

Vessels), a deployable real-time adaptive dynamics identifi-cation and control system. MOSAIC-SV identifies a control-sufficient dynamics model from a spec-sheet engineering prior, without dedicated identification trials, re-estimating it at every control step of a closed-loop mission while the controller plans on it. Additionally, the system is robust to poor engineering priors, remaining effective when the inertia or damping prior is wrong by an order of magnitude. MOSAIC-SV rests on two further contributions:  

> •

A physically admissible unscented Kalman filter over hydrodynamic, disturbance, and actuator parameters with a command-dependent consider projection that suppresses corrections along weak directions of the prior-scaled joint actuator and external-force map.  

> •

An evaluation in simulation under static mismatch, transient changes, and prior misspecification; paired field trials on a small twin-thruster catamaran against its frozen engineering prior; and a feasibility run on a 6.3-tonne dual outboard monohull. A. Parameter Definition 

Taking vech as an operation that stacks a lower-triangular matrix into a vector, we define the estimator parameters from the vessel’s dynamics model as 

θ = [ vech( LM ), vech( LD ), c, F ext , θ a ]⊤ ∈ R21 (10) where LM and LD are the lower-triangular Cholesky factors of M and Dlin , respectively, c ∈ R3 parametrizes the quadratic-damping coefficients through q = c ⊙ c, and Fext 

and θa are the force-bias and actuator-deviation parameters. We floor the diagonal entries of both LM and LD to a small positive constant when reconstructing M and Dlin ,making the inertia and damping matrices positive definite by construction and ensuring that M is invertible. The Cholesky factor representation therefore guarantees dissipative linear damping and avoids indefinite matrices that can arise from estimating matrix values directly. The smooth parameteriza-tion q = c ⊙ c guarantees nonnegative quadratic-damping coefficients.

Due to limitations with vessel availability, comparisons to other models were not conducted on this vessel. VI. DISCUSSION 

A. Self-Prediction and Transit Time 

As shown in Table II, self-prediction error and transit time do not necessarily correlate. For example, under mass mismatch MOSAIC-SV transits 25 .2% faster than the cal-ibrated model while having 17 .3% higher self-prediction error. Alternatively, under external current it predicts 23 .2% 

better while transiting 5.5% slower. This is because the two metrics answer different questions: consistency of the belief vs. quality of the resulting plan. Self-prediction measures whether the model’s one-second predictions match what the vessel did, while transit time measures whether the com-mands those rollouts executed were good over the mission. A small, stable error in the model minimally impacts transit time with a controller that re-plans every step; alternatively a slow model may track well but result in a conservative plan. 

B. Trends Across Perturbations 

MOSAIC-SV performs best when the mismatch lies in directly excited parameters with a wide prior (Table II). For example, inertia is excited by every acceleration along its axis and carries a wide prior (§III-B), and mass mismatch is where MOSAIC-SV gains most over the calibrated model (Table II). Alternatively, the current is represented as the force bias 

Fext , which §III-C treats as a fast state reverting toward zero, and under external current MOSAIC-SV transits 5.5% slower than the calibrated model while predicting 23 .2% better. A fast bias is generally beneficial, as it absorbs whatever the hydrodynamic blocks cannot explain and lets the controller act on it quickly; however, reversion also means a persistent current is partly forgotten and re-learned throughout the run. 

C. Limitations 1) Sim-to-Real Gap: Two effects were observed on water that are not present in the simulated plant. First, the vessel’s yaw response was weaker than its engineering model pre-dicts. The differential thrust produced less turning moment than the lever-arm geometry implies, which was absorbed into the estimator as yaw damping and lever-arm parameters. Second, the thrusters have a start-up dead time and a low-command dead band, which are poorly explained by any constant-gain model and which the estimator attributes to a phantom force. Neither effect is present in the simulation runs: the simulated plant produces exactly the yaw moment its lever-arm geometry implies, and its thrusters have no start-up delay or dead band. 

2) Innovation Aliasing: An innovation is the mismatch between the predicted and observed transition, and several parameters can explain the same mismatch. For example, within the force parameters, a thrust shortfall and an op-posing surge force produce the same surge innovation. The consider projection of §III-D withholds the correction when the current command cannot separate them, but is evaluated per interval and not over the entire run. Therefore the estimated parameters are control-sufficient but are not literal measurements of the vessel hydrodynamic parameters. This is also why the out-of-model sim-to-real effects land in their respective blocks. VII. CONCLUSIONS We present MOSAIC-SV, a deployable real-time adaptive control system that can identify a control-sufficient dynamics model, without dedicated identification trials, and plan on it. In simulation, the system recovers the transit performance of a calibrated model under static mismatch or transient changes and tolerates order-of-magnitude scaling of its in-ertia and damping priors. On water, MOSAIC-SV transited faster and predicted its own motion better than a frozen prior in every paired run, including under an unmodeled payload. The same system concept was also feasibly deployed to control a 6.3-tonne vessel. Future work will extend the trials to further hull and actuator classes, propagate the parameter covariance into the MPPI rollouts so the controller can hedge along unresolved directions, and extend the formulation from 3-DoF to 6-DoF to account for vessel stability.

## Safe Position and Attitude Control for Landing on Small Celestial Bodies with Gravitational Uncertainty 

Felipe Arenas-Uribe 1,∗, T. Michael Seigler 1 and Jesse B. Hoagg 1

> 1

Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, University of Kentucky, Lexington, KY, USA 

Correspondence*: felipearur@uky.edu 

ABSTRACT 

Landing on small celestial bodies is challenging because the spacecraft must satisfy safety and actuator constraints despite gravitational forces that are difficult to model accurately. This article presents an optimal control for safe landing with gravitational uncertainty and strict actuator limits. The approach addresses spacecraft pose control with attitude represented on SO(3) , where there are actuator constraints and state constraints on position, attitude, velocity, and angular velocity. The approach combines several key techniques. First, the gravitational force uncertainty is estimated using an extended high-gain observer, and we present a new dynamic upper bound on the estimation error. This gravitational estimate and dynamic bound are then used to compute optimal control forces and torques that satisfy state and actuator constraints while tracking a landing trajectory. Optimal forces and torques are obtained from the closed-form solution to a quadratic program that has a single control barrier function constraint constructed by composing multiple control barrier functions that are designed to enforce each state and actuator constraint. Finally, an optimal allocation maps control forces and torques to actuator commands that satisfy actuator constraints. The method is demonstrated in simulation using 2 vehicle configurations with severe gravitational uncertainty. 

> Keywords: Spacecraft control, constrained control, optimal control, control barrier functions, polytope projection

1 INTRODUCTION 

Small celestial bodies (SCBs) have attracted attention for their scientific value and relevance to planetary defense. Sample return missions provide insights into the formation and composition of celestial bodies. Example missions include NASA OSIRIS-REx, JAXA Hayabusa, and ESA Rosetta. In addition, near-Earth objects motivate active SCB monitoring, and the rapidly developing space economy has generated interest in space mining (Modell, 2025). Soft landing on SCBs is a proximity maneuver that requires precise control of position and attitude, while satisfying actuator limits and safety constraints (Zhang and Duan, 2023). Spacecraft actuator systems are typically overactuated to provide redundancy as a passive fault-tolerant strategy against actuator failures (Hasan et al., 2022). Nevertheless, soft landing is a challenge because of the uncertain exogenous force due to disturbances such as solar radiation pressure and unmodeled gravitational effects (Mammarella et al., 2025). Error in the mass estimate for a SCB is typically in the range of 10–15% (Bull et al., 2021). In addition, SCBs have irregular shapes and nonuniform mass distribution. To account for these effects, higher-order models can be used (Scheeres, 2012). However, accurately determining model parameters requires high-resolution remote sensing or in-situ measurements that are expensive to acquire (Pe˜ narroya and Paoli, 2019). Even in the best case, the gravitational field model has residual error, which needs to be addressed by the control system. Spacecraft guidance and control often uses a modular architecture, where the guidance module generates a reference trajectory and the feedback controller is responsible for following the reference. Spacecraft pose control is addressed using a variety of representations, including position-attitude on R3 × SO(3) , dual quaternions, and the special Euclidean group SE(3) (Zhang and Duan, 2023). Spacecraft pose control methods include integrated, adaptive, and disturbance rejection approaches (Wang and Ji, 2019; Sun and Zheng, 2018; Zhang et al., 2022; Zhang and Duan, 2023). For position-only control, robust approaches such as H∞ (Simpl´ ıcio et al., 2018), sliding mode (Fu et al., 2022), and adaptive (Tiwari et al., 2023; Sun and Zheng, 2018) have been developed. Linear extended high-gain observers (EHGO) have also been applied to estimate and compensate for gravitational uncertainties (Dunham et al., 2016). Although these approaches can improve robustness and reference tracking performance, they generally do not provide guarantees of safety constraint satisfaction. On the other hand, model-predictive control (MPC) can explicitly incorporate state and input constraints. MPC methods have been developed for spacecraft proximity operations using classical and dual-quaternion-based formulations (Tiwari et al., 2022; Van Leeuwen et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2021). However, these methods typically rely on approximations and discretizations of the nonlinear spacecraft dynamics. Consequently, guaranteeing recursive feasibility and constraint satisfaction often requires constraint-tightening and robustness mechanisms, which can significantly increase computational complexity. These limitations motivate the use of control barrier functions (CBFs), which provide a framework to enforce state constraints through forward invariance (Wieland and Allg¨ ower, 2007; Ames et al., 2019). CBF methods for spacecraft have been developed for orbit transfers (Ong and Ames, 2023), proximity operations (Breeden and Panagou, 2023), and formation flying (Kamat et al., 2026). However, uncertainty in the gravitational field makes SCB landing a challenging application for CBF methods. Although robust CBF approaches (Jankovic, 2018) address model uncertainty, synthesizing a valid CBF subject to actuator constraints is difficult, and model uncertainty only exacerbates this challenge. An alternative approach is to estimate the model uncertainty using a disturbance observer (Das and Murray, 2022; Alan et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2024; Alan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Das ¸ and Burdick, 2025). However, these approaches rely on bounds on disturbance derivative to characterize observer error, and more importantly, they do not address the challenge of actuator constraints. This article presents a new control method for safe landing on a SCB with gravitational uncertainty. The approach addresses spacecraft pose control with attitude represented on SO(3) , where there are strict state constraints on position (i.e., glideslope constraint), attitude (i.e., boresight constraint), velocity, and angular velocity, as well as strict actuator constraints. This safe landing approach relies on several key building blocks. First, Section 4 uses a polytope projection to map the set of admissible actuator inputs into a lower-dimensional set of admissible forces and torques. This process transforms the control variable from actuator inputs to control forces and torques, and transforms the actuator constraints into equivalent constraints on the control force and torque. This process is analogous to Durham (1994). This section then presents an optimal control allocation to map admissible forces and torques back to admissible actuator commands. Next, Section 5 presents a nonlinear EHGO to estimate gravitational uncertainty. Notably, we develop a dynamic upper bound on the estimation error, which is critical for guaranteeing safety. This section also uses the gravitational force estimate to develop desired control forces and torques for tracking the reference trajectory, which is assumed to be generated and periodically updated by a guidance module. Section 6 presents the main contribution of this article, which is an optimal feedback control for safe landing that satisfies all state and actuator constraints. Although the desired control forces and torques achieve landing, they do not generally satisfy state and actuator constraints. Thus, we present a method inspired from (Rabiee and Hoagg, 2024a,b) to construct a composite CBF that can be used to ensure all state and actuator constraints are satisfied. The approach in (Rabiee and Hoagg, 2024a,b) does not address

Note that the dynamic bound is less conservative than a standard robust CBF approach (Das ¸ and Burdick, 2025). Then, the desired forces and torques and the composite CBF are used to construct a closed-form optimal control that achieves landing while satisfying state and actuator constraints. This safe and optimal control is the solution to a quadratic program (QP) that can be expressed in closed form, which is computationally efficient for on-spacecraft implementation. Finally, Section 7 presents simulation studies using 2 vehicle configurations: a robotic lander similar to the Hayabusa2 mission (Ho et al., 2021), and a crewed landing module similar to the Apollo lunar module (Grumman Aerospace Corporation, 1969). However, this article goes beyond the preliminary work by addressing position-and-attitude control rather than position-only control.

8 CONCLUSIONS 

This article presents a control architecture for safe soft-landing maneuvers of overactuated and fully actuated spacecraft on small celestial bodies under gravitational uncertainty. Several key contributions enable simultaneous position and attitude control while satisfying state and input constraints. The constraint projection method presented in Section 4 is a key enabler to re-frame the actuator constraint 

u ∈ U as a constraint on the forces and torques μ ∈ Φ. Thus, allowing to focus on designing control forces and torques instead of individual actuator commands. Moreover, the allocation presented in (18) provides a mapping from the forces and torques to the actuator inputs given by a quadratic program which Proposition 1 proves to be feasible and has a unique solution. It is worth noting that other allocation algorithms may be used to perform such mapping provided that they enforce the constraint u ∈ U .The gravitational uncertainty estimation ˆw presented in Section 5 is an essential element of the control architecture. It is not only used to compensate for the disturbance in (24). But also, by leveraging on δ

and Proposition 2, we design a computable lower bound of the CBF constraint function (44) as shown in Proposition 7. Finally in Section 6, we present a minimum-intervention control which is shown to be continuous in Proposition 9 and Theorem 1 shows that it satisfies (O2) and (O3) while producing a control that approximates the desired control (26), which is designed to achieve (O1). The simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method enables successful landings under significant gravitational uncertainty where traditional feedback control fails. Furthermore, the closed-form nature of the controller ensures the computational efficiency required for autonomous onboard implementation. A limitation of the polytope projection method is the growth in the number of half-spaces ℓ, which depends on the geometry of U and the allocation matrix M . We note that the projection of any convex set through the affine mapping (9) is a convex set, indicating that the input constraint projection method can be extended to more general convex sets. However, finding efficient representations for Φ when U is a non-polytopic convex set is open for future work.

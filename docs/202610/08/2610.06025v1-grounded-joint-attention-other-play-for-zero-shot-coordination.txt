Abstract 

Joint attention – the human ability to share a common visual or cognitive focus with others – enables a meeting of minds 

that lets us coordinate even with unfamiliar partners. In this work we investigate whether equipping AI agents with a sim-ilar mechanism can enable such zero-shot coordination. We introduce Mutual Attention for zero-shot TE aming (MATE): a novel multi-agent reinforcement learning method inspired by human joint attention. MATE encourages agents to coordinate their actions by aligning their visual attention on scene-salient objects during the interaction rather than relying on arbitrary partner-dependent conventions established during training. Unlike symmetry-breaking approaches that merely prevent brittle conventions from emerging, MATE actively promotes coordination through an environment-grounded signal that is naturally shared across partners. We evaluate MATE on three benchmarks: our Card Alignment Game, designed to isolate brittle convention formation, and the more challenging Level-Based Foraging and OvercookedV2 benchmarks. Our experi-ments consistently show that a joint-attention-inspired signal improves coordination with unknown partners, underlining MATE’s potential as a general coordination mechanism that complements and surpasses symmetry-breaking approaches. 

# 1 Introduction 

One of the most common approaches for training agents in multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) is self-play 

(SP) (Tesauro 1995), in which agents learn by interact-ing with a copy of themselves. Despite yielding strong re-sults in competitive (Silver et al. 2016; Berner et al. 2019; Vinyals et al. 2019; Baker et al. 2020) and cooperative set-tings (de Witt et al. 2019; Hu and Foerster 2020; Yu et al. 2022), agents trained via SP struggle to generalise to novel partners (Carroll et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2020; Strouse et al. 2021). This motivates research on zero-shot coordination 

(ZSC): coordinating with unseen partners without prior co-ordination (Stone et al. 2010; Hu et al. 2020). ZSC per-formance is usually evaluated in cross-play (XP) by pair-ing agents from independent training runs. Performance de-grades substantially (Hu et al. 2020) when agents approach the same task through incompatible arbitrary conventions that break environment symmetries during training (Foerster et al. 2019). To solve this issue, humans use joint attention as a social signal to dynamically resolve ambiguity and estab-lish the relevant object of joint action (Tomasello 2014; Car-penter and Call 2013). In the card example, humans would look at their intended card and track their partner’s gaze to successively converge on a joint choice. We hypothesise that joint attention could play a similar role for AI agents and ask: 

Can joint attention serve as a coordination signal for independently trained partners at test time? 

We propose Mutual Attention for zero-shot TE aming (MATE), a novel multi-agent reinforcement learning method arXiv:2610.06025v1 [cs.AI] 5 Oct 2026 inspired by human visual joint attention. Each agent receives its partner’s attention as a coordination signal and conditions its policy on it, making attention interactive: Agents do not merely learn to align their attention on the same object, but also to use the partner’s visual focus to guide their own at-tention and actions as the interaction unfolds (see Figure 1). We build MATE on top of other-play (OP) (Hu et al. 2020) that targets brittle convention formation by exploiting known environment symmetries: Rather than coordinating with an identical copy, an agent is trained with a randomly symmetry-permuted version of itself, preventing convergence on arbi-trary conventions. In the card example in Figure 1, OP can prevent run-specific choices, such as always selecting purple or green, but it does not determine which card agents should choose: While OP removes one source of failure, it does not provide any within-episode signal for selecting one among several equally plausible cards. MATE supplies this missing signal through the partner’s visual attention, allowing agents to coordinate where OP alone cannot. We evaluate MATE first in our Card Alignment Game 

introduced above, which isolates this coordination task in its simplest form, and then in two established coordina-tion benchmarks: Level-Based Foraging (Albrecht and Ra-mamoorthy 2013) and OvercookedV2 (Gessler et al. 2025). Our results suggest that successful ZSC may require not only preventing incompatible arbitrary conventions, but also en-abling agents to negotiate coordination during execution by establishing a shared attentional focus. This positions MATE as a method for fostering generalisable coordination strate-gies that transfer to novel partners. Our contributions are: 1. We introduce MATE, a joint attention training mecha-nism built on top of OP, in which each agent observes its partner’s visual attention and learns to act on it. 2. We identify coordination problems in which enforcing symmetry-equivariant policies alone is insufficient, and introduce the Card Alignment Game as a minimal testbed that isolates this limitation. 3. We show that MATE outperforms competitive baselines in the Card Alignment Game as well as Level-Based Foraging and OvercookedV2, together with qualitative evidence that coordination stems from mutual attention rather than brittle conventions. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Zero-Shot Coordination 

ZSC investigates the ability of agents to coordinate with inde-pendently trained partners at test time, without co-adaptation. This is challenging when a task admits multiple equivalent but mutually incompatible optimal joint policies. We focus on this setting rather than broader ad-hoc teamwork (AHT), where agents collaborate with previously unknown partners whose behaviours may initially be unknown (Stone et al. 2010), as AHT is commonly treated as a natural next step once ZSC performance improves (Dizdarević et al. 2025; Ruhdorfer et al. 2026). Existing ZSC methods primarily ad-dress this coordination problem by discouraging arbitrary symmetry breaking during training. OP (Hu et al. 2020) independently transforms each agent’s Dec-POMDP using sampled environment relabellings, preventing the arbitrary conventions that SP would otherwise form around environ-ment symmetries. However, its performance is bounded by the symmetries the environment admits. Off-Belief Learn-ing (Hu et al. 2021) on the other hand is an algorithm that prevents arbitrary conventions and improves ZSC by tak-ing as input a reference policy π0 and optimizes a new unique policy π1 under the assumption that previous ac-tions were taken by π0 while future actions will be taken by 

π1 using a belief model. More recently, Forkel et al. (2026) showed that high entropy regularisation promotes symmetry-equivariant and XP-compatible policies, with High-Entropy (HE) IPPO, a standard IPPO with an unusually large entropy coefficient, achieving state-of-the-art inter-seed XP score in Hanabi. These approaches share the premise that coordina-tion can be recovered by suppressing arbitrary conventions. This is not always sufficient. When several possible refer-ents remain symmetric within the current episode, removing arbitrary conventions does not by itself determine what the agents should coordinate around. We instead equip agents with a coordination signal grounded in the episode itself, namely shared attention, that remains informative even when no symmetry is left to exploit. 

2.2 Joint Attention as a Coordination Signal 

Joint attention is the shared and mutually recognised focus of two individuals on the same object or event. It is a foun-dational mechanism in human social cognition (Tomasello 2014; Mundy and Jarrold 2010; Carpenter and Call 2013) and has been studied for coordination in human-robot in-teraction (Staudte and Crocker 2011; Anzalone et al. 2019; García-Martínez et al. 2024). In MARL, Lee et al. (2021) equip agents with spatial attention heads and an intrinsic reward that penalises divergence between their attention maps, encouraging them to attend to the same elements of a shared observation; this improves SP coordination in gridworld tasks. However, because their evaluation is lim-ited to SP, the shared-attention incentive may act as an ad-ditional convention-forming channel: co-trained agents can simply learn to align their attention on an arbitrary, possibly task-irrelevant location to initiate a coordinated behaviour, without producing a signal that transfers to independently trained partners. To leverage communication, prior work in MARL learns protocols for exchanging task-relevant infor-mation (Foerster et al. 2016; Sukhbaatar, Szlam, and Fer-gus 2016; Mordatch and Abbeel 2018), but these often fail to transfer to independently trained partners (Bullard et al. 2020; Tucker et al. 2021). MATE instead uses spatial atten-tion as an environment-grounded communication channel. Without a pre-established convention, two factors matter be-yond aligned attention: agents need to be mutually aware that they share a focus (Carpenter and Call 2013) and the focus needs to be on a relevant object (Csibra and Gergely 2009). MATE addresses both limitations by enforcing attention to be not only aligned but also focused on objects, yielding an observable signal for establishing an episode-specific target with novel partners. 3 Preliminaries

# 4 Mutual Attention for Zero-Shot Teaming

We call this method MATE , which equips OP-trained IPPO agents (de Witt et al. 2020; Schulman et al. 2017) with a model of joint attention. Each agent shares its focus with its partner and learns to steer its own attention toward the partner’s future focus and on task-relevant objects.

# 5 Experiments 

We first test MATE in the Card Alignment Game, a toy envi-ronment that we propose to validate the core intuition behind our method in isolation. We then evaluate MATE in two more complex domains, Level-Based Foraging (LBF) (Al-brecht and Ramamoorthy 2013) and OvercookedV2 (Gessler et al. 2025), which require agents to agree on a shared target and infer coordination from their partner’s actions.

5.3 OvercookedV2 

OvercookedV2 (Gessler et al. 2025) introduces partial ob-servability and asymmetric information into Overcooked, one of the most widely used ZSC benchmarks. We extend the JaxMARL (Ruther-ford et al. 2024) environment with image observations and, following Gessler et al. (2025), apply OP by independently permuting the two recipe ingredients in each agent’s obser-vation. While Gessler et al. (2025) embed each local view in a zero-padded global grid, we centre the observation on one agent and crop it to the view radius to reduce dependence on fixed layout coordinates and improve generalisation (Ye et al. 2020). Partner attention is shared only within the receiver’s visible region, preventing leakage of private recipe informa-tion yet making OvercookedV2 particularly challenging for MATE, as attention can provide a coordination signal only intermittently. Task-relevant kitchen objects, including car-ried ingredients, form the object set used by the attention loss. 

# 6 Results 

We evaluate whether MATE improves ZSC by comparing SP and XP return (Hu et al. 2020), with XP computed from dis-joint pairs of independently trained policies following Forkel et al. (2026). Across all environments we compare IPPO, OP, HE IPPO (Forkel et al. 2026) and MATE, additionally includ-ing the joint-attention baseline of Lee et al. (2021) in the Card Alignment Game. Results are averaged over 48 seeds in the Card Alignment Game and 12 in LBF and OvercookedV2. OP and MATE are trained under the same permutation for a fair comparison; no permutations are applied during evalu-ation. All methods are evaluated at the checkpoint with the highest training return. For HE IPPO, we always report the SP and XP scores at the entropy coefficient achieving the highest XP return in the sweep shown in Figure 7. 

6.1 Card Alignment Game 

In the Card Alignment Game (Figure 3a), IPPO solves SP but falls to chance in XP, showing that independently trained agents learn incompatible conventions. OP rules out brittle conventions, but without a coordination signal, it remains at chance in both SP and XP. HE IPPO also fails: none of the tested entropy coefficients yields XP above chance, and large coefficients collapse non-greedy SP performance (Figure 7). The Lee et al. (2021) joint-attention objective behaves sim-ilarly: varying β over three orders of magnitude leaves the outcome unchanged, with perfect SP but chance XP. MATE instead obtains high return in both settings ( 0.872 ± 0.008 

SP, 0.823 ± 0.010 XP), moving substantially above chance while keeping the SP-XP gap small. Figure 4 shows the cor-responding attention patterns. MATE agents progressively align attention on the same card and select it, yielding a strat-egy that transfers to independently trained partners. Under OP alone, attention remains agent-specific and final choices do not align. 

MATE Ablation Study We analyse how each component of MATE contributes by individually removing OP (w/o OP), the partner-attention input (w/o feed), and the object-level auxiliary loss (w/o aux); see Figure 3a. MATE w/o OP rein-troduces brittle conventions, resulting in perfect SP but chance-level XP, while w/o aux at chance level demon-strates that joint attention is insufficient without ground-ing: agents still exchange attention, but an ungrounded signal carries no meaning a novel partner can recover. MATE w/o feed performs equivalently to OP. All three com-ponents, symmetry-robust training, interaction-time atten-tion sharing, and object-level grounding, are thus necessary. 6.2 Level-Based Foraging 

In LBF (Figure 3b), IPPO exhibits a clear SP–XP gap, drop-ping from 0.356 ± 0.057 in SP to 0.229 ± 0.068 in XP. OP reduces this gap but also lowers SP performance, while HE IPPO improves both SP and XP without fully closing the gap. MATE performs best in both settings, reaching 0.460 ±0.002 

in SP and 0.446 ± 0.003 in XP. Attention maps in Figure 5 show the coordination mech-anism. In LBF, several apples are simultaneously equally valid targets and the time budget is short: agents must con-verge within a few steps, or the episode is lost to wasted moves. MATE partners align their combined attention on the next apple and collect it; OP agents, instead, each attend to their own vicinity and never commit to a shared target in time. 

6.3 OvercookedV2 

In Demo Cook Simple (Figure 3c), MATE achieves the high-est XP return, reaching 132 .2 ± 3.0 in XP and 143 .3 ± 1.4

in SP. IPPO drops from 167 .6 ± 8.3 in SP to 104 .2 ± 17 .8

in XP. HE IPPO reaches the highest SP return in the sweep (208 .6), but only 120 .6 ± 51 .2 in XP (see Figure 7), leav-ing a substantial SP–XP gap; even its best-XP configuration remains below MATE at 125 .3 ± 14 .4. OP improves XP to 

116 .8 ± 10 .9, considerably above the 47 ± 15 .2 originally reported for OP by Gessler et al. (2025). In our experiments, this gain comes from replacing zero-padded observations with agent-centred crops: with the original representation, agents develop additional conventions tied to fixed spatial positions, suggesting that ingredient relabelling alone may not capture all relevant symmetries. We therefore report the original result only as a reference rather than as a directly comparable baseline. The benefit MATE can provide in this layout is inher-ently constrained. The agents’ fields of view overlap for only 

262 ± 9 of the 400 evaluation timesteps and, even then, the shared region covers on average 11 ± 0.3 cells and at most 

15 of the 50 cells in each view, limiting how often mutual attention is effective. Figure 6 illustrates one such brief win-dow. Once the informed agent returns to the pot carrying the required ingredient, the ingredient reveals the hidden recipe, while mutual attention directs the uninformed agent’s focus towards this cue, indicating that the prepared soup should be delivered. Nevertheless, MATE outperforms OP under the same agent-centred observations and ingredient permu-tations, showing that it can improve coordination even under constrained shared visibility, and extending its benefits to partial observability and asymmetric information. 

# 7 Discussion and Limitations 

Our results show that MATE supports within-episode coor-dination across tasks requiring target agreement, as in Card Alignment and LBF, and interpretation of behaviour under partial observability, as in OvercookedV2. This suggests that some coordination problems cannot be solved by suppress-ing arbitrary conventions alone; for these, grounding coor-dination in signals recoverable within the episode offers an effective route to generalisation in cooperative multi-agent systems. A limitation is that MATE assumes access to a detector for task-relevant objects during training. We also evaluate only with AI partners; whether the learned attention signal stays interpretable and useful in human–AI coordina-tion remains open. 

# 8 Conclusion 

We introduced MATE, a multi-agent reinforcement learn-ing method that models joint attention as a general zero-shot coordination mechanism between independently trained partners. Across three diverse environments, MATE closes the SP-XP gap where prior zero-shot coordination meth-ods remain at chance level. Future work could extend this idea beyond shared focus, using attention-based objectives and attention-avoidance to support richer forms of coordina-tion, including division of labour, help-seeking, warning, and complementary attention to different task-relevant objects.

A.2 Hyperparameters 

Our codebase builds on JaxAHT (Wang et al. 2026), and we ran our experiments on Ubuntu servers equipped with NVIDIA Tesla V100-SXM2 GPUs with 32 GB of memory. A.3 High-Entropy IPPO Baseline 

Following Forkel et al. (2026), we keep standard IPPO train-ing unchanged and tune the entropy coefficient over a larger range (Figure 7). This should make independently trained policies compatible, with XP matching SP. In the Card Align-ment Game, we extend the sweep to 10 5, yet the greedy SP– XP gap remains open throughout. In LBF, greedy SP and XP become close for several coefficients, but never intersect, with SP consistently higher. In OvercookedV2, greedy XP approaches and eventually exceeds SP only after increasing entropy has substantially degraded SP performance.

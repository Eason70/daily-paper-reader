Abstract —Hamilton–Jacobi reachability constructs safety cer-tificates for specified dynamics and safety constraints, tying each certificate to the deployment context for which it is synthesized. We ask whether a single certificate can instead represent afamily of context-dependent safety problems and be queried across deployment conditions without re-synthesis. We learn a backward reachable tube for an eight-state vehicle model conditioned on local boundary geometry, friction coefficient, and adversarial disturbance scale. Geometry enters through an ego-frame boundary observation that defines the local con-tainment constraint, while friction and disturbance scale enter as explicit operating-condition variables. This allows the same value function to be queried across friction coefficients from 0.4 to 2.0 and on geometries absent from synthesis. On 11 held-out evaluation geometries, the certificate maintains containment across the full tested friction range, including simultaneous geometry and grip shifts, while remaining within 1.2 percentage points in intervention rate and 0.09 m/s in speed of certificates re-synthesized with knowledge of the test geometry. We then deploy the certificate as a sampled discrete-time control barrier function filter on a full-scale vehicle near the handling limit. Lateral containment holds in every hardware session under both adversarial driving and autonomous racing, with 99th-percentile acceleration magnitude reaching 0.99 g. Across three certificates evaluated under a fixed autonomous racing controller, lap time varies by only 3.1%, demonstrating that a context-conditioned reachability certificate can transfer to deployment geometries absent from synthesis with modest performance cost. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Safety-critical control depends on knowing not only which states are safe, but under which conditions they are safe. For a vehicle operating near the limit of tire adhesion, those conditions can change substantially across deployments: track geometry determines the admissible region, while tire–road friction determines which states can still be recovered within that region. A safety certificate synthesized for one deployment is therefore tied to the context in which it was constructed. Hamilton–Jacobi (HJ) reachability makes this dependence explicit. Given a dynamics model, safety constraint, and dis-turbance model, HJ reachability computes a value function whose zero level set separates states from which the constraint can be maintained from those from which it cannot [1], [2]. Changing the deployment context can consequently change the reachable set itself. For a vehicle, this occurs not only when the track changes, but also when the available grip differs from the value assumed during synthesis. We learn a backward reachable tube for an eight-state ve-hicle model conditioned on local boundary geometry, friction coefficient μ, and adversarial disturbance scale sd. Geometry is provided as an ego-frame boundary observation, allowing the same value function to be queried at runtime on geometries absent from synthesis. This formulation makes it possible to distinguish the context assumed by the certificate from the conditions encountered by the vehicle, providing a direct way to study transfer under geometry and grip mismatch. We test the learned certificate in simulation on held-out track geometries and across a range of friction levels, including simultaneous changes in geometry and grip. We compare against certificates re-synthesized for the target geometry or grip condition, quantifying the safety and performance cost of avoiding context-specific synthesis. The central finding is that context conditioning makes reachability transferable with-arXiv:2610.08115v1 [eess.SY] 6 Oct 2026 out making it overly conservative: one certificate maintains containment across the tested contexts while staying close in intervention rate and speed to certificates specialized to the test condition. We then deploy the resulting certificate as a sampled discrete-time control barrier function filter on a full-scale vehicle at a skidpad (Fig. 1). The filter is exercised both by a professional driver instructed to approach the boundary and by an autonomous racing controller. Rather than treating absence of violations as the sole measure of success, we characterize both containment and the cost of transfer, including the intervention required to maintain containment and the resulting performance cost. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of context-conditioned HJ reachability for transferable safety certificates across deployment conditions, validated on a full-scale vehicle at the handling limit. II. R ELATED WORK 

Vehicle safety at the handling limit. At the limit of grip, vehicle safety is commonly enforced by restricting the vehicle to a set of states considered recoverable, either as constraints in predictive control or as triggers for shared control [3]– [7]. On full-scale vehicles, Dallas et al. [8] filter steering and drive commands during drifting using a closed-form safe set with a fixed friction coefficient, identifying adaptation to model uncertainty as an open problem. Closest to our setting, concurrent work learns a control barrier value function by boundary sampling along Pontryagin characteristics and deploys it as a filter for full-scale shared-control drifting [9]. These works demonstrate safety filtering near the handling limit, but their certificates are synthesized for a specific deployment condition. 

Learned reachability and its guarantees. Neural reacha-bility methods have made high-dimensional reachable sets tractable [10]–[12], including gradient-free formulations that propagate discrete-time Bellman–Isaacs values without requir-ing spatial value gradients [13]. Because a learned value function is an approximation, its zero level set is not acertified safe set; scenario optimization and conformal pre-diction supply probabilistic correction bounds for learned tubes [14], [15]. Such guarantees are typically formulated for specified dynamics and constraints, so extending them across a continuous context space requires additional treatment. We therefore treat our learned tube as an uncertified certificate, report containment empirically, and regard formal verification as complementary. 

Conditioning and transfer of safety certificates. Parameter-conditioned reachability represents families of reachable sets by lifting scalar problem parameters, such as disturbance mag-nitude or obstacle size, into the state [16], [17]. Learned safe sets have also been conditioned on perceptual observations of unknown surroundings, for quadrupedal navigation [18] and for simulated racing from ego-vision [19]. In these works, the conditioning captures aspects of the surrounding environment while the underlying dynamics remain fixed. Our setting couples two distinct forms of conditioning: local boundary geometry determines the safety constraint, while friction and disturbance scale determine the recoverable envelope under the vehicle dynamics. This combination couples changes in the safety constraint with changes in the recoverable envelope within a single learned representation. On full-scale vehicles, prior reachability-based filtering has used grid-computed sets over low-dimensional state spaces and has been demonstrated in ordinary driving rather than at the handling limit [20]. Model mismatch has instead typically been addressed by adapting the dynamics, for example through friction estimation [21], [22] or learned corrections to anominal model [23], or by robustifying the barrier condition against parametric uncertainty [24], [25]. These approaches modify the model or margin used by the controller; they do not characterize how a learned reachability certificate transfers when deployment conditions differ from those assumed during synthesis. In contrast, our formulation retains a single learned certificate and exposes the relevant deployment conditions as queryable context, allowing transfer to be evaluated directly against certificates re-synthesized for the target condition. This gap is particularly consequential near the handling limit, where modest changes in grip or vehicle dynamics can change the recoverable set. We therefore evaluate transfer both in simulation, across geometry and grip shifts, and on a full-scale vehicle operating near the friction limit. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

We consider a family of safety problems indexed by a context c ∈ C . The context collects quantities that are fixed over the reachability horizon but vary across deployments. In shared-control racing, these include environment structure, such as track geometry and boundary layout, and operating conditions, such as available grip and disturbance authority. Problem 1 (Context-conditioned safety filtering) . Given a family of context-indexed safety problems {f (·; c), ℓ (·; c)}c∈C ,construct a runtime safety filter that, for a queried context cq

V. A PPROACH 

A. Context-conditioned reachable tube 

Problem 1 asks for a safety filter that adapts across a family of context-indexed safety problems. A fixed reachable tube would certify one such problem: one track geometry, one assumed friction coefficient, and one disturbance model. We therefore approximate this family jointly with a single context-conditioned value function. Let x := ( z, c ). We define the augmented dynamics: 

xk+1 = [ f (zk, u k, d k; c); c] , (8) so the context is held fixed over each reachability rollout. The safety margin is lifted similarly as ℓ(x) = ℓ(z; c).The resulting value function Vθ (x, k ) can be queried at a particular context to obtain the corresponding safety envelope for that geometry, grip, and disturbance assumption. The filter therefore adapts its safety envelope to the queried context rather than enforcing a single envelope that is simultaneously conservative across all contexts. The context variables play different roles. Friction coef-ficient and disturbance scale are low-dimensional operating conditions, whereas track geometry is environment structure that must generalize across layouts. We therefore do not pro-vide the track index directly to the value network; instead, we encode the local boundary geometry through an observation map, described next.

Our simulation study evaluates whether one context-conditioned certificate can replace separate certificates synthe-sized for each track and grip condition. We ask four questions: (i) whether the same certificate generalizes to unseen track geometries, (ii) whether it transfers across the conditioned grip range, (iii) whether geometry and grip shifts compound when they occur together, and (iv) what happens when the certificate is queried at the wrong grip. A. Simulation Setup 

We simulate the vehicle model of Sec. IV with a plant friction coefficient μtrue . The safety filter is queried at μq ,which equals μtrue unless stated otherwise. The nominal controller is a curvature-feedforward racing-line tracker with PD lateral and PI speed feedback. It is deliberately detuned so that, without filtering, it overdrives the tires and leaves the corridor. The same nominal controller, initial conditions, and filter parameters are used in every comparison.

Speed is the mean longitudinal speed over completed trials only, and is reported as n/a when no trials complete.

C. Generalization across geometry 

We next isolate the geometry axis. We hold grip at the iden-tified μ0 = 0 .92 and evaluate on the same 11 geometries used to define the in-distribution and out-of-distribution training splits. For the out-of-distribution model, these geometries are absent from the training library. Table III reports the results. The nominal controller leaves the corridor on every trial. The context-conditioned filter removes all violations on ge-ometries it was never trained on. Its cost is also nearly identical to the geometry-specialized upper bound: O URS intervenes only 0.1 percentage points more often than O NE TRACK and runs within 0.09 m/s. Thus, in this setting, conditioning on local boundary geometry allows one certificate to generalize across tracks with almost no loss relative to re-synthesizing on the test geometry.

E. Simultaneous geometry and grip shift 

Finally, we shift geometry and grip together by running the grip sweep of Table IV on the 11 held-out geometries. This is the most direct test of the context-conditioned claim: the certificate must adapt to an unseen track while also being queried at different grip levels. Table V shows that the two shifts do not compound into failures: O URS maintains containment for every held-out ge-ometry and every grip value. Its cost remains close to the geometry-specialized reference, staying within 1.2 p ercentage points of intervention and 0.09 m /s of O NE TRACK at every grip. The comparison to O NE GRIP is more revealing: O URS

C. Safety Filtering on an Autonomous Racing Controller 

The adversarial protocol exercises the certificate but does not quantify its performance cost, since it does not involve timed laps. We therefore also evaluate the filter with an autonomous racing controller: the nominal tracking controller of Sec. VI-A, following a minimum-curvature racing line. This experiment extends the evaluation in Table III to hardware, allowing us to quantify the performance cost of deploying the certificate on a geometry absent from its training set. 

1) Two geometries, three certificates: We drove two skid-pad figures. The oval is a loop of constant-radius turns and short straights; the bean is an asymmetric closed curve whose curvature varies continuously. Table VI reports every distinct drive on the two. On the bean, the held-out and in-distribution certificates produce nearly identical performance. Mean lap time differs by 0.08 s , mean speed by 0.09 m /s, and intervention by 

0.1 p ercentage points. On the oval, OOD has a mean lap time 

0.35 s shorter than I ND and 0.23 s longer than F INETUNED ;the three certificates span 3.1 % in mean lap time, with OOD also reaching the highest peak speed at 18 .43 m /s. Consistent with the simulation results in Table III, the held-out certificate therefore incurs little additional performance cost relative to certificates specialized to the deployment geometry. Figure 3 also exposes a model mismatch that is not visible from containment alone. The measured acceleration envelope does not coincide with the envelope predicted by the model used by the certificate. The model overpredicts the measured envelope over much of the operating range but underpredicts it near the handling limit. The discrepancy is strongly di-rectional: the largest gap occurs near −90 ◦, corresponding to the loaded lateral direction on the counterclockwise oval. The maximum directional gap is 3.22 m /s2 for F INETUNED ,

2.91 m /s2 for OOD, and 1.92 m /s2 for I ND. Thus, the de-ployment demonstrates containment despite model mismatch in the high-load regime relevant to the experiment. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a context-conditioned neural Hamilton–Jacobi reachable tube that represents a family of safety problems across track geometry, friction, and disturbance conditions, and deployed it as a runtime safety filter on a full-scale vehicle near the friction limit. In simulation, a single value function transferred across held-out geometries and the conditioned friction range without re-synthesis, including simultaneous geometry and grip shifts. On hardware, lateral containment held in every session, including trials in which a professional driver deliberately attempted to leave the corridor. The hardware results further demonstrate that this transfer can be achieved with modest performance cost. Over ten autonomous laps under a fixed racing-line tracker, lap time varied by 3.1 % across certificates trained on in-distribution, held-out, and fine-tuned geometry sets. The held-out and in-distribution certificates also produced comparable performance on the two hardware geometries, both of which were absent from the held-out certificate’s training library. Together with the simulation results, these experiments show that a single context-conditioned certificate can transfer across deployment geometries and operating conditions without the need for context-specific re-synthesis. Several limitations remain. The certified property is lateral containment of the vehicle center of gravity; rollover, thermal degradation, tire wear, and containment of the full vehicle footprint are outside the specification. The full-scale evaluation is also limited to two skidpad geometries—an oval and a bean-shaped course. Thus, while the simulation study evaluates transfer across a broader geometry library, the hardware results should be read as deployment validation rather than as evi-dence of transfer across all track families. Finally, the hardware mismatch experiments test only an interior range of queried grip values: containment held over the tested range, but the first failures observed in simulation occurred at the next mismatch levels, which were not evaluated on the vehicle. Extending the hardware study to lower-grip surfaces and diverse full-scale geometries would provide a more complete characterization of context-conditioned certificate transfer. IX.

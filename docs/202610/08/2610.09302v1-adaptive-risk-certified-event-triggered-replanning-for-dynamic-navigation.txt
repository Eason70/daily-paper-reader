Abstract: Safe navigation in dynamic environments requires robots to plan un-der obstacle predictions whose errors are uncertain, non-stationary, and can in-duce rare but safety-critical failures. Existing control-barrier-function safety fil-ters can reject immediately unsafe controls, but they provide little guidance on when the current finite-horizon planning mode itself is becoming unsafe as predic-tion uncertainty evolves. We propose Conformal Event-Triggered Risk-Certified Replanning ( CERT-Replan ), a framework that uses calibrated barrier risk as an early-warning signal for replanning. CERT-Replan calibrates horizon-indexed obstacle-prediction residuals online and uses the resulting uncertainty radii to evaluate dynamic-obstacle safety margins. A one-step safety filter protects the next applied control, while a horizon-level risk monitor evaluates the upper-tail CVaR of predicted barrier-violation losses along the current MPC rollout. When this risk exceeds an allocated budget, CERT-Replan rejects the current planning mode and selects a lower-risk alternative, such as a different speed profile, corridor, or homotopy class, rather than repeatedly correcting the same nominal plan. In a non-stationary benchmark, CERT-Replan achieves an 83 .3% collision reduction relative to the safety-filter-only baseline and a 77 .8% reduction relative to simple replanning triggers, while reducing average safety-filter intervention by 32 .4% .With Trajectron++, CERT-Replan achieves 96% collision-free operation. Hardware experiments and onboard runtime profiling demonstrate computational feasibility. 

Keywords: Mobile Robot Navigation, Event-Triggered Replanning, Conformal Prediction, Risk-Aware Safety, Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance 

# 1 Introduction 

Autonomous robots operating in crowds, warehouses, sidewalks, and mixed-traffic environments must make local planning decisions from imperfect predictions of nearby agents. A typical navigation stack estimates obstacle states from onboard sensing, predicts short-horizon obstacle motion, computes a local plan, and applies a safety filter before execution. Certifying this pipeline is challenging because obstacle-prediction errors are rarely stationary or uniform: they can grow under occlusion, close interaction, dense traffic, abrupt intent changes, and changes in sensing geometry. Modern multi-agent predictors capture rich interaction patterns, but their uncertainty can be multi-modal and can shift as the robot enters new sensing or interaction regimes [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. As a result, a nominal forecast may be accurate in benign scenes but unreliable in rare, safety-critical tail events. Control barrier functions (CBFs) provide a principled optimization-based mechanism for rejecting im-mediately unsafe controls [9]. For dynamic-obstacle navigation, however, the safety margin depends not only on the robot state but also on uncertain future obstacle states. Dynamic parabolic CBFs (DPCBFs) improve dynamic-obstacle avoidance by incorporating relative velocity in a line-of-sight frame, thereby reducing the conservatism of purely distance-based or collision-cone-like constraints

Robust, distributionally robust, and perception-aware CBFs further address model, sensing, and obstacle-state uncertainty [11, 12, 13, 14]. Yet these filters are fundamentally local in time: they protect the next executed input, but a nominal or one-step filtered control does not by itself indicate whether the current finite-horizon planning mode is becoming unsafe as prediction uncertainty evolves. Risk-aware safety filters partially address this issue by replacing pointwise safety constraints with constraints on the distribution of safety margins. Conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) barrier functions protect against low-probability, high-severity events by constraining the adverse tail of the safety-margin distribution [15, 16], and risk-adaptive CVaR methods tune the risk level online to balance conservativeness and feasibility [17]. These methods, however, still require uncertainty samples or error models that remain representative under distribution shift. Adaptive conformal inference (ACI) provides a complementary mechanism by recalibrating uncertainty online from recent prediction residuals without assuming a fixed parametric error model [18, 19]. Conformal planning has been used to construct calibrated prediction regions for dynamic environments [20], and recent work has extended conformal ideas to adaptive POMDP planning, recursively feasible MPC, and time-aware planning in dynamic scenes [21, 22]. In this paper, we use online conformal calibration as the data-driven adaptation mechanism that supplies horizon-indexed uncertainty radii to both immediate safety filtering and horizon-level plan evaluation. The key gap we address is temporal. A one-step safety filter can repeatedly modify unsafe controls, but repeated correction of the same nominal plan may still push the robot toward regions where future feasible controls are scarce or unavailable. Similarly, resolving the same MPC problem at every step does not answer the higher-level question of when the robot should change the local reference, speed profile, corridor, homotopy class, or risk allocation. Event-triggered replanning provides the appropriate abstraction: update the plan only when a task-relevant condition indicates that the current mode should no longer be trusted [23, 24, 25]. Our central idea is to use calibrated barrier risk as this condition. Rather than treating risk only as a constraint on the next input, CERT-Replan uses it as an early-warning signal for replacing the current local planning mode. This paper makes four contributions: 1. We introduce a horizon-indexed adaptive conformal calibration mechanism for dynamic-obstacle prediction errors, producing online position and velocity uncertainty radii that are shared by immediate safety filtering and horizon-level mode evaluation. 2. We formulate an ACI+Adaptive-CVaR-DPCBF safety filter that uses calibrated geometric tightening and lower-tail CVaR constraints to protect the next applied control under non-stationary prediction uncertainty, while adapting the CVaR tail mass to preserve feasibility. 3. We propose a finite-horizon CERT risk monitor that evaluates the current local planning mode through upper-tail CVaR of predicted barrier-violation losses, yielding a conditional risk certificate for the evaluated mode. 4. We integrate this certificate into an event-triggered MPC replanning layer that replaces the current mode when its allocated horizon-risk budget is exceeded, and validate the resulting framework in randomized simulations and hardware-in-the-loop experiments. 

# 2 Related Work 

Local planning and replanning. Classical reactive and local-planning methods include dynamic-window approaches, graph replanning, sampling-based motion planning, and trajectory optimiza-tion [26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. These methods can react to dynamic scenes, but they typically require additional uncertainty handling to provide probabilistic safety under prediction errors. Risk-aware planning methods incorporate chance constraints, distributional robustness, coherent risk measures, or sampling-based risk estimates to account for uncertain obstacle motion [32, 33, 34]. Event-triggered and self-triggered control methods update communication or control actions only when needed to preserve system-level properties under resource or uncertainty constraints [23, 35, 36]. 2Event-triggered MPC and learned replanning policies reduce unnecessary replans and can improve navigation efficiency, but existing triggers are often based on tracking error, feasibility, map updates, or learned heuristics rather than calibrated tail risk [24, 25]. In contrast, our trigger is tied to a calibrated finite-horizon risk certificate for the current local planning mode. 

Prediction uncertainty and conformal planning. Multi-agent trajectory prediction is often multi-modal due to latent goals and interactions [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Conformal prediction gives distribution-free finite-sample coverage under exchangeability [37, 38], while adaptive conformal inference updates the miscoverage level online to handle distribution shift [18]. In robot motion planning, conformal prediction has been used to construct prediction regions for dynamic obstacles [20], adaptive conformal prediction has been used for online planning among dynamic agents [19, 39], and recent work has extended conformal ideas to time-aware dynamic-environment planning and CBF safety certification [40, 41]. Our method differs by using calibrated residuals inside a risk-sensitive barrier filter and by using horizon-level tail risk as a replanning trigger. 

Risk-aware CBFs. CBFs have become a standard tool for safety-critical control because they can be embedded in optimization-based controllers [9, 42]. CVaR-CBFs extend this idea to stochastic safety and protect against poor-tail outcomes [15]. Risk-adaptive CVaR barrier functions adjust the CVaR level online to avoid unnecessary conservativeness while preserving feasibility [17]. Recent safety-filter and predictive-control methods combine CBFs with differentiable optimization, sampling-based MPC, learned neural barriers, and conformal prediction for uncertainty-aware safety certification [43, 44, 45, 41]. DPCBFs improve dynamic-obstacle avoidance by shaping the safe set with both relative position and relative velocity [10]. Closely related MPC-CBF and predictive-CBF methods encode barrier conditions over a prediction horizon and provide feasibility or safety analyses under modeled dynamics and constraints [46, 47, 48]. Our framework is complementary: rather than proposing a new nominal DPCBF or MPC-CBF formulation, we use adaptive conformal calibration and CVaR barrier risk to decide when the current local planning mode should be replaced under non-stationary obstacle-prediction uncertainty.

# 5 Simulation and Experimental Results 

Figure 2 illustrates the comparison of CERT-Replan in two simulated scenarios, we additionally evaluate the framework with learned multi-modal prediction, and Figure 3 shows the hardware experiment. The primary simulations use 100 randomized trials per scenario in a 16 × 50 workspace with moving obstacles whose positions and velocities are subject to uncertainty. The hardware experiment uses 10 trials, with dynamic obstacles injected virtually for repeatability. A trial is successful if the robot reaches the goal without violating the safety radius. Otherwise, the trial is counted as a failure due to collision or timeout. Collision indicates a robot-obstacle safety violation, and timeout indicates that the robot exceeds the episode length of 60 s . When success and collision rates do not sum to 100% , the remaining trials are timeouts. We report path length over successful trials and use ∥uk − ¯uk∥2 to measure average safety-filter intervention, where ¯uk is the nominal MPC input and uk is the filtered input. Additional implementation details, including the vehicle model, obstacle predictor, residual sampling, candidate mode generation, and parameter settings, are provided in Appendix F. Hardware implementation details and experiment-specific parameters are described in Appendix G. 

Crowded Dynamic Environment. The dense-crowd environment contains 30 moving obstacles and tests persistent interaction under prediction noise. Table 1 shows that nominal DPCBF fails frequently, with 19% success and 81% collision, while TEB improves success to 85% but still collides in 15% 

of trials. AAC-DPCBF reaches 96% success and 4% collision, showing that conformal uncertainty calibration and adaptive tail-risk filtering substantially improve the one-step safety filter. The ablation in Table 3 of Appendix E further shows that these components are complementary: ACI+DPCBF reduces collision from 81% to 20% , Adaptive-CVaR-DPCBF improves success to 76% , and their combination gives the strongest safety-filter-only result. CERT-Replan achieves the best overall performance, with 97% success and 3% collision, while reducing average safety-filter intervention by 34 .2% . The gain in success rates over AAC-DPCBF is modest because this scene mainly stresses local obstacle avoidance rather than strategic mode switching; Fig. 2(a) shows that nearby alternatives often have similar risk.

±0.75 m . Table 2 shows that nominal DPCBF fails under this shift, with 3% success and 97% 

collision. TEB improves success to 66% , but still collides in 34% of trials. AAC-DPCBF reaches 

79% success and 12% collision, showing that conformal calibration and adaptive CVaR improve robustness under shifted prediction errors. The ablation in Table 3 of Appendix E clarifies the role of each component. Adaptive-CVaR-DPCBF without conformal calibration remains close to nominal DPCBF, with only 4% success under the nonstationary shift, while ACI+DPCBF recovers 58%

Combining both components gives the strongest safety-filter-only baseline, confirming that calibrated uncertainty scaling and adaptive tail-risk filtering are complementary. To isolate the effect of the replanning criterion, we additionally compare CERT-Replan against Slack-Replan and a rate-matched Periodic-Replan. All three replanning methods use the same MPC planner, AAC-DPCBF filter, candidate modes, obstacle realizations, and paired random seeds, and only the trigger differs. CERT-Replan achieves 86% success and 98% collision-free operation (95% Wilson CI: [93 .0, 99 .4] ), outperforming both Slack-Replan and Periodic-Replan. These gains are significant under paired McNemar tests ( p ≤ 0.039 ). Periodic-Replan and CERT-Replan have similar replanning frequencies ( 38 .5 vs. 39 .1), indicating that the gain is associated with trigger timing rather than more frequent replanning. CERT-Replan also outperforms the long-horizon conformal-planning baseline ICP, improving success from 80% to 86% and reducing collision from 20% to 2% . As shown in Fig. 2(b), the risk-certified trigger activates in the transition and heavy-tailed regions, allowing the planner to switch to a lower-risk candidate mode before one-step filtering becomes insufficient. This further demonstrates the benefit of CERT-Replan: when the current local plan accumulates high predicted horizon risk, the framework can switch to a lower-risk candidate mode before one-step filtering becomes insufficient. Additional sensitivity analyses for the prediction horizon and empirical CVaR sample count are provided in Appendix H. 

Learned Multi-Modal Prediction. To evaluate CERT-Replan beyond the constant-velocity predictor, we instantiate the same framework with the official Trajectron++ multi-modal predictor [3]. All 

K = 20 weighted prediction modes are used in both AAC-DPCBF filtering and CERT horizon-risk evaluation. No other component of the safety or replanning framework is changed. Over 100 nonstationary trials, CERT-Replan achieves 96% collision-free operation and reduces collision incidence by 86.2% relative to nominal DPCBF and by 91.3% relative to TEB. These results demonstrate that CERT-Replan operates directly on learned multi-modal prediction samples rather than depending on the constant-velocity model used to isolate the risk-calibration mechanism in the primary experiments. 

Experiment Results. We evaluate CERT-Replan on a Husarion ROSbot 2 Pro with a Jetson Orin Nano. As shown in Fig. 3, nominal DPCBF collides in all trials, while AAC-DPCBF improves the success rate to 60% with a 20% collision rate but produces the longest successful-run path. CERT-Replan performs best, achieving 90% success and 10% collision while reducing successful-run path length relative to AAC-DPCBF. The trajectory snapshots and closest-obstacle distance plot show that CERT-Replan changes its local plan near the high-risk region and maintains positive clearance after the critical interaction, suggesting that the horizon-risk trigger reduces reliance on conservative filter-only corrections by shifting part of the safety response to the planner. Additional representative real-robot snapshots are provided in Fig. 4 in Appendix G, and extended videos are included in the supplementary material.

# 6 Limitations 

The finite-horizon risk monitor is a conservative diagnostic based on Markov’s inequality, upper-tail CVaR, and a union bound. It certifies the evaluated planning mode, not closed-loop conformal safety. A distribution-free closed-loop guarantee would require additional assumptions on prediction errors, sampling, and replanning. The primary experiments use a constant-velocity predictor to isolate the effect of conformal calibration and risk-certified replanning, while Sec. 5 additionally evaluates the framework with learned multi-modal prediction. In the current simulations, obstacles avoid one another but do not react to the robot. Therefore, mode changes do not alter the residual-generating obstacle policy. Robot-responsive interactions may introduce additional prediction shift after replanning and are not covered by the present conditional certificate. Future work will evaluate standardized crowd-navigation benchmarks and experiments with physical moving agents. 

# 7 Conclusion 

We presented CERT-Replan, an event-triggered replanning framework that treats the local planning mode, rather than only the next control input, as the object to be certified under non-stationary obstacle-prediction errors. The central idea is to use calibrated barrier-risk information not only for immediate safety filtering, but also as an early-warning signal for when the current finite-horizon mode should no longer be trusted. By monitoring tail risk along the predicted rollout, CERT-Replan can replace a high-risk mode with a lower-risk alternative before safety filtering becomes overly corrective or infeasible. This shifts dynamic-obstacle navigation from purely reactive control correction toward risk-aware mode management. Simulations and onboard hardware experiments show that this design improves robustness under distribution shift and reduces collisions relative to nominal, safety-filter-only, and simple-trigger baselines.

# E Safety-Filter Ablation Studies 

We perform an ablation study to isolate the contribution of each safety-filter component before adding risk-certified replanning. All methods use the same MPC planner, robot dynamics, DPCBF parameters, and obstacle scenarios. They differ only in how the DPCBF constraint handles prediction uncertainty and tail risk. The nominal DPCBF baseline uses the predicted obstacle state without conformal tightening or CVaR risk filtering. ACI+DPCBF adds adaptive conformal inference to the nominal DPCBF by tightening the barrier with calibrated position and velocity radii, but does not use CVaR. Adaptive-CVaR-DPCBF uses adaptive CVaR tail-risk filtering without conformal prediction or conformal tightening. AAC-DPCBF combines both components, using adaptive conformal calibration together with adaptive CVaR-DPCBF filtering. This ablation isolates the effect of conformal uncertainty calibration, adaptive CVaR tail-risk filtering, and their interaction under otherwise identical planning and control settings. Table 3 shows that both ACI and adaptive CVaR improve over nominal DPCBF, but their roles differ. In the dense-crowd setting, ACI+DPCBF strongly reduces collision by inflating the barrier under 15 prediction uncertainty, while Adaptive-CVaR-DPCBF improves success with smaller average control intervention by focusing the filter on lower-tail barrier risk. Combining both gives the strongest safety-filter-only result, improving success to 96% and reducing collision to 4% .The nonstationary setting highlights the importance of calibration. Adaptive-CVaR-DPCBF without ACI performs similarly to nominal DPCBF, indicating that risk adaptation alone cannot compensate for a shifted or miscalibrated prediction-error distribution. ACI+DPCBF recovers much of the lost performance by adapting the uncertainty radii online, and AAC-DPCBF further improves success to 79% while reducing collision to 12% . These results support the design of the proposed safety filter: conformal calibration provides reliable uncertainty scaling, while adaptive CVaR shapes the controller response to tail-risk events.

Residual sampling. For empirical CVaR evaluation, obstacle samples are generated by perturbing the nominal forecast with residual samples from the corresponding horizon-indexed windows. For horizon step τ , position and velocity perturbations are sampled from Wpk,τ and Wvk,τ , respectively. 

Candidate modes. The candidate mode set Πk contains local alternatives to the current MPC mode. In our implementation, candidates are generated by modifying the nominal reference through speed scaling, lateral-offset detours, and a closest-obstacle-behind detour. The speed-scaled modes use vref ∈ { 1.0, 0.75 , 0.5, 0.25 , 0}vref nom , where the zero-speed mode corresponds to yielding. The lateral-offset modes shift the local reference by ±1.0 m and ±1.5 m relative to the nominal path when a feasible corridor exists. The closest-obstacle-behind mode constructs a detour that passes behind the nearest high-risk obstacle, where “behind” is defined with respect to the obstacle’s predicted velocity direction. Each candidate is evaluated by solving MPC and computing the horizon risk Rk(π). If at least one candidate satisfies the CERT constraints, the lowest-cost certified candidate is selected. If no candidate is certified, the fallback mode is the candidate with minimum Rk(π).
